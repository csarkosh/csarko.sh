# How a small-town street intersection is built

**Question:** How is a real street intersection in an American small town built, from the curb radius to the paint and the signs? And what does someone drawing one for a game need to get right?

**Short answer:** An intersection is one shared pavement bounded by curb returns, not two streets overlapping. The corner radius sets how fast a car turns and how far a person walks. Ramps with a yellow dome panel carry the sidewalk around each corner. Paint and signs say who goes, and on a quiet residential street they often say nothing. Tees, skews, alleys, dead ends, highways, gravel and rails each change the parts. The figures come from Washington's law, WSDOT's manual and plans, and the federal MUTCD.

**What that leaves open:** Most figures here are guidance ("should") or one agency's practice, and the doc says which. No source read here measures a pre-war corner, how two crossing streets' crowns are blended, or how far a storm inlet sits from a crosswalk. By the end you can tell a requirement from a habit, and the gaps are listed so nobody takes one for a rule.

The figure draws one four-way corner from numbers this doc cites: look at how the curb return, the ramps and the crosswalk bars stack up from the roadway to the lot line, and where the stop line and the STOP sign sit on the side street.

**In numbers:**

- **20 ft and 30 ft:** how far Washington law holds a parked car from a crosswalk, and from the approach to a stop sign ([section 3](#paint-says-who-goes-and-the-law-keeps-parked-cars-20-ft-from-a-crosswalk-whether-it-is-painted-or-not)).
- **10 ft, 25 ft and 30 ft:** the corner radius Seattle sets between two neighbourhood streets, and Bremerton's minimums for two residential streets and for any other meeting ([section 1](#an-intersection-is-one-shared-pavement-bounded-by-curb-returns-and-the-corner-radius-sets-how-fast-cars-turn-and-how-far-people-walk)).
- **4 ft, 8.3% and 2 ft:** a curb ramp's minimum width, its steepest running slope and the depth of its dome panel ([section 2](#the-sidewalk-turns-the-corner-on-ramps-4-ft-wide-8-3-at-most-with-a-2-ft-dome-panel-at-the-foot)).
- **1,000 units a day:** the volume under which the federal manual lists "no intersection control" as a possible choice for a quiet corner ([section 4](#signs-say-who-stops-the-minor-street-gets-the-stop-sign-and-a-quiet-residential-corner-often-has-none)).

## 1. An intersection is one shared pavement bounded by curb returns, and the corner radius sets how fast cars turn and how far people walk

Stand at a corner and look at the curb: the side street's curb does not run straight into the main street's. It sweeps round in an arc, and the sidewalk follows it. The arc is the curb return, and the pavement between the four returns is one surface that both streets share. Washington law defines the intersection area as the area within the prolongation or connection of the lateral curb lines of the roadways that join [1], and the federal manual defines it the same way [2]. The returns, not the streets' centre lines, bound it.

The size of that arc is a trade. WSDOT's Design Manual says to minimize the conflict area as much as possible while still providing for the design vehicle, to control the speed of turning vehicles and reduce the exposure of everyone else [3]. Larger turn radii for large vehicles mean more pavement and longer pedestrian crossings, so WSDOT suggests accommodating a large vehicle, which lets it encroach on other lanes, instead of designing for it [3].

How big the arc is depends on which streets meet, and the published numbers are one agency's choice each. Seattle's standard sets the curb radius at 10 ft (3.0 m) where two neighbourhood streets meet, no more than 20 ft (6.1 m) for corners of other street types, and 25 ft (7.6 m) or 30 ft (9.1 m) where truck streets meet; its standard design vehicle is an SU-30 with a 42 ft turning radius [4]. Seattle also notes that the radius a car sweeps, the effective radius, can be larger than the curb's when parking or bike lanes sit beside it [4]. Bremerton sets a minimum of 25 ft (7.6 m), measured from the radius point to the face of the curb, only where two local access residential streets meet; 30 ft (9.1 m) at any other meeting, including a residential street and a collector; and 40 ft (12.2 m) or more where large trucks and buses turn often [5]. On a state highway, WSDOT's initial ranges for a right-turn corner give a radius to the edge of the traveled way of 30 ft (9.1 m) for a passenger car, 50 ft (15.2 m) for an SU-30 truck or a city bus, and 55 ft (16.8 m) for a WB-40 truck [3]. A game that draws one radius everywhere is drawing one agency's preference as a law.

The curb itself is plain. WSDOT's standard curb-and-gutter section stands 6 in (0.15 m) above the gutter, sits on a gutter 6 in thick, and is 1 ft 6 in (0.46 m) wide overall; the monolithic curb-and-sidewalk detail shows a 4 in (0.10 m) sidewalk behind a curb face 6 in high [6], [7]. These are WSDOT's drawings, not every town's.

A note on which edition of the federal manual this doc reads. Washington adopted the 11th Edition of the MUTCD in WAC 468-95-010, filed on Sep 26, 2025 and effective on Oct 27, 2025, with revisions incorporated "except as may be modified herein" [8], [9]. The 11th Edition has a Revision 1 dated December 2025, which FHWA calls the current edition as of Nov 22, 2026 [10]. Every MUTCD figure here was read in the December 2023 text and compared with Revision 1, and none of the sections cited changed a figure; where Revision 1 reworded a paragraph, the section number is the same.

The returns settle where the pavement ends. The sidewalk behind them has to turn the same corner, and it has to dip to the street to do it.

## 2. The sidewalk turns the corner on ramps: 4 ft wide, 8.3% at most, with a 2 ft dome panel at the foot

Look down at the foot of a corner ramp: a rectangle of yellow bumps, set right against the curb. The statutory ramp requirement is old (section 7); WSDOT's rule for new construction is a curb ramp oriented in each direction of pedestrian travel within the width of the crosswalk it serves, which makes two per corner [11]. The ramp must be matched at the far end of the crossing unless there is no curb or sidewalk there [11].

WSDOT names three layouts. A perpendicular ramp cuts through the curb and meets the gutter at a right angle, with its landing at the top; on a small-radius corner it lines up better with the crosswalk, and on a larger radius it gives a blind pedestrian less to find direction by. A parallel ramp runs along the walk with its landing at the bottom and fits a narrow right of way. A combination ramp joins the two, and its landings may be shared [11].

The numbers are requirements. WSDOT's manual states them as "shall": a clear width of 4 ft (1.2 m) at least, excluding flares; a running slope of 8.3% (1:12) at most, and if that would make the ramp longer than 15 ft (4.6 m), a ramp at least 15 ft long that may be steeper; a cross slope of 2.1% at most; a landing 4 ft by 4 ft (1.2 m) at least; flares 10% at most; a counter slope in the gutter of 5% at most; and a clear area 4 ft by 4 ft in the roadway beyond the foot, within the crosswalk's width and outside the parallel travel lane [11]. The federal access guidelines (PROWAG) give the same width, running slope and its 15 ft exception, cross slope, landing and clear area [12]. WSDOT's perpendicular-ramp plan draws the same figures, and notes that at a marked crosswalk the connection between ramp and roadway must lie within the width of the crosswalk markings [13]. A 6 in curb at the 8.3% limit takes a ramp about 6 ft long, since 0.5 ft divided by 0.083 is 6.0 ft; that is arithmetic from two cited numbers, not a figure from a drawing.

The sidewalk behind has its own floor. WSDOT's minimum sidewalk is 5 ft (1.5 m) excluding the curb, and a buffer between curb and walk should be at least 3 ft (0.9 m) wide, with a planted one typically 4 ft (1.2 m) at least; for a street posted at 25 mph in a suburban setting its suggested widths are a 6 ft sidewalk with a 4 ft buffer, or a 6 ft sidewalk, a 2 ft buffer and parking [11]. Those suggested widths are what the figure draws.

The panel at the foot is the detectable warning surface. A truncated-dome panel must extend at least 24 in (0.61 m) in the direction of travel and the full width of the ramp run or landing, and it sits against the back of the curb with no more than a 2 in gap [12], [14]. The domes have a base diameter of 0.9 to 1.4 in, a height of 0.2 in, and a centre-to-centre spacing of 1.6 to 2.4 in [12]. The panel is yellow by one agency's choice and not by a federal colour rule. PROWAG requires only that it contrast with the surface around it, light on dark or dark on light [12]. WSDOT says that federal yellow is the colour it uses to achieve that contrast on its projects, and that within cities other contrasting colours may be used if the city asks [11]. At a driveway the panels are required on both sides only where the driveway is controlled by a stop or yield sign or a signal [11].

Two things stay out of the ramp. Gratings, access covers and other fittings may not sit on ramps, landings or the gutters within the pedestrian route [11]. Instead, WSDOT's drainage exhibit puts a catch basin or inlet in the gutter on the upstream side of the ramps, and says to consider more where water could pond; it gives no distance from the crosswalk [11]. The slope across the crossing is limited too: PROWAG sets 2.1% at most across a crosswalk at an approach with a stop or yield sign, and 5% across an uncontrolled approach [12]. So the street that has the stop sign is flat where people cross it, and the through street may be tilted.

The ramps have to land inside the crosswalk lines, which is the paint's job.

## 3. Paint says who goes, and the law keeps parked cars 20 ft from a crosswalk whether it is painted or not

Walk across a side street where no stripes were ever painted and you are still in a crosswalk. Washington defines a crosswalk as the portion of the roadway between the intersection area and a prolongation or connection of the farthest sidewalk line, or, with no sidewalk, a line ten feet from the intersection area, "except as modified by a marked crosswalk" [15]. A driver must stop and remain stopped for a pedestrian in a marked or an unmarked crosswalk, and may not pass a vehicle stopped for one at a marked crosswalk or at an unmarked one at an intersection [16]. WSDOT says the same in its manual: legal crosswalks exist at all intersections, whether marked or not [11]. The MUTCD adds that at a non-intersection location the markings legally establish the crosswalk [17].

When a crosswalk is marked, the federal rules are specific. The markings shall be white, and where transverse lines are used they shall be 6 to 24 in (150 to 610 mm) wide; a marked crosswalk shall be at least 6 ft (1.8 m) wide; and where curb ramps are provided, the markings shall be located so that the ramps are within them [17]. The "ladder" and bar crosswalks have bars 12 to 24 in wide, spaced 12 to 60 in apart and no more than 2.5 times the bar's width (a "shall") [17]. WSDOT's own standard goes wider and plainer: a marked crosswalk at least as wide as the walkway leading to it or 10 ft (3.05 m), whichever is greater, with the ladder bar preferred [11]. WSDOT's layout sheet draws bars 2 ft (0.61 m) wide with a gap of 5 ft (1.52 m) at most, kept out of the wheel paths where possible, and sets the stop line ahead of the bars [18]. Whether to mark at all is a judgement. On approaches controlled by a STOP or YIELD sign the manual says markings should be installed where engineering judgment indicates they are needed to direct pedestrians [17], and on a state highway WSDOT's Region Traffic Engineer makes the call [11].

The stop line comes next. A stop line is a solid white line across the approach lanes; the manual says it should be 12 to 24 in (305 to 610 mm) wide and, at a controlled intersection, placed at least 4 ft (1.2 m) in advance of the nearest crosswalk line [17]. With no marked crosswalk, the manual's guidance puts the line at the desired stopping point, not more than 30 ft or less than 4 ft from the nearest edge of the intersecting traveled way; Washington amended that paragraph so that the line goes where drivers should stop, "in no case less than 4 feet" from the intersecting roadway, and dropped the 30 ft ceiling [17], [19]. A stop line is not used where drivers only yield to a YIELD sign [17]. Stop lines may be staggered lane by lane, which the manual says can improve the driver's view of pedestrians, give better sight distance to turning vehicles and increase the turning radius for left turns [17].

Then the centre line. It is yellow; a normal line is 4 to 6 in (100 to 150 mm) wide, and the manual says a broken line should be 10 ft segments with 30 ft gaps [17]. WSDOT draws it at 4 in wide with the same 10 ft and 30 ft [20]. A street needs one by rule only if it is busy: centre lines shall be placed on a paved undivided urban arterial or collector with a traveled way of 20 ft or more and an ADT of 6,000 or more, should be placed from 4,000, and call for engineering judgment on a traveled way less than 16 ft wide [17]. A quiet residential street in a small town can therefore have none. Washington requires edge lines on principal and minor arterials in urbanized areas, except where a curb or sidewalk exists [21]. Where a centre line does run to an intersection, the manual's standard is that an extension of it through the intersection shall be a dotted line, and its guidance is 2 ft segments with gaps of 2 to 6 ft; WSDOT's sheet draws a dotted extension line at 2 ft with a 6 ft gap [17], [20]. The manual does not say how far short of the crosswalk the broken line stops, and this research found no figure for it.

Last, the parking that is held back from the corner. Washington's statute keeps vehicles from stopping, standing or parking in an intersection or on a crosswalk; from standing or parking, except momentarily to pick up or drop off, within 20 ft (6.1 m) of a crosswalk and within 30 ft (9.1 m) upon the approach to a flashing signal, stop sign, yield sign or traffic signal at the side of the roadway; within 15 ft (4.6 m) of a fire hydrant; and in front of a driveway or within 5 ft (1.5 m) of the end of the curb radius leading to it [22]. The federal manual lets a town paint the curb for these statutory distances without word markings or signs [17]. So a corner with no paint on its curb still has its no-parking zone: the zone is the statute, and the paint only repeats it.

None of that says who goes first. Signs do.

## 4. Signs say who stops: the minor street gets the STOP sign, and a quiet residential corner often has none

A corner of two quiet streets may carry nothing but street names. With no sign, Washington's rule is the old one: when two vehicles approach or enter an intersection from different highways at about the same time, the driver on the left yields to the one on the right [23]. The federal manual lists what may support choosing no control at all: adequate sight distance on every approach; single-lane approaches with no turn lanes; combined vehicle, bicycle and pedestrian volume entering the intersection under 1,000 units a day or 80 in the peak hour; no marked crosswalks or bicycle lanes; no approach that is a through highway, main road or higher functional class; an intersection angle between 90 and 75 degrees; and two local streets, or a local street and a collector [24]. Those are factors that "may be considered", an option and not a rule.

A STOP sign goes on the minor street. The manual's guidance for choosing the minor road is that it intersects a designated through or numbered highway, has the lower functional classification, the lower volume or the lower speed limit; where two streets are about equal, control the direction that conflicts most with pedestrian crossings or school routes, has obscured vision, or has the best sight distance from the stopped position [24]. Minor-road stop control is considered where a view is restricted, crashes warrant it, or a lower-class road meets a higher-class one [24]. A full stop on every approach needs an engineering study, and meeting a warrant does not by itself require it [24]. A YIELD sign is the lighter choice and is never placed on every approach except at a roundabout [24].

Where the sign stands is specified. A STOP sign shall be on the near side of the intersection on the right-hand side of the approach, as close as practicable to the intersection while keeping it visible; it should be no farther than 50 ft (15.2 m) from the edge of the pavement of the intersected road, and, where there is a marked crosswalk, in advance of the crosswalk edge nearest approaching traffic [24]. Where two roads meet at an acute angle the sign should be turned or shielded so its face is out of view of traffic it does not control [24]. Its minimum size is 30 by 30 in (0.76 m) on a single-lane conventional road and 36 by 36 in (0.91 m) on a multilane one [24]. In a residential area the bottom of a roadside sign shall be at least 7 ft (2.1 m) above the top of the curb, or above a sidewalk [25]. For a post, the guidance is a lateral offset of 12 ft from the edge of the traveled way, but where a residential street has limited sidewalk width the manual allows at least 1 ft (0.3 m) from the face of the curb, and supports should not intrude into the usable width of the sidewalk [25]. In the figure the sign stands 1 ft behind the curb face, in the verge.

The street-name sign is nearly always there. The manual's guidance is a Street Name sign at all street intersections in urban areas; in residential areas at least one at each intersection, naming both streets, with its faces parallel to the streets it names, and in business areas at least on diagonally opposite corners [26]. A street name may be placed above a STOP sign with no required vertical separation, so the blade can share the STOP sign's post [26]. Post-mounted letters on a two-lane street are 6 in tall for the initial capital and 4.5 in for lower case, and on a two-lane local street at 25 mph or less 4 in and 3 in may be used [26].

A STOP sign and its stop line, the crosswalk and the clear curb together make the usual small-town corner. Some corners do not fit it.

## 5. Not every junction is a cross: the T, the skew, the alley mouth and the dead end each change the parts

**The T.** A three-way junction has one fewer crossing. WSDOT's manual puts crosswalks normally on all legs, three at a T and four at a four-leg, with a minimum of two at a T and three at a four-leg for continuity of the walking route [11]. The side of the through street opposite the stem has no intersecting approach, and there the manual allows a solid edge line to run across the far side of the T, where it would otherwise be discontinued across an intersecting approach [17]. Which leg stops is decided by the criteria in section 4.

**The skew.** Two streets can meet at a slant. WSDOT's desirable intersection angle is 90 degrees, with 60 to 120 degrees allowed, and no angle points in the through roadway within the intersection or within 100 ft (30 m) of the edge of a crossroad's traveled way [3]. The federal manual's list of conditions for yield control or no control uses a narrower range, 90 to 75 degrees [24], and Bremerton's table gives 80 to 90 degrees for an intersection [5]. So WSDOT allows a wider range of angles than the manual's conditions for yield or no control: a crossing at 60 degrees is inside WSDOT's limit and outside the manual's 75. At a slant the manual suggests dotted lane-line extensions for offset, skewed or complex intersections, and lets stop lines stagger [17]. Seattle notes that its 10 ft default has exceptions for angled streets where 10 ft cannot be achieved [4].

**Offset streets.** Where a cross street's two legs do not line up, Seattle says to consider supplementary markings when the lane offset through the intersection exceeds 3 ft (0.9 m) [4]. Bremerton's standard keeps the centre lines of adjacent streets at least 160 ft (49 m) apart for local and collector streets in any combination, and 300 ft (91 m) where a local or collector street crosses an arterial [5].

**The alley's mouth.** An alley is not a street here. Washington's statute says the junction of an alley with a street or highway does not constitute an intersection, and WSDOT's rule amends the MUTCD's definition to the same effect [1], [27]. A driver emerging from an alley or driveway in a business or residence district must stop immediately before the sidewalk, or the sidewalk area extending across the alley, and yield to a pedestrian [28]. WSDOT builds that in: an alley or driveway does not break the sidewalk's continuity, so the sidewalk design typically continues across it [11]. Its Type 1 entrance carries the sidewalk across at full width between two pedestrian ramps of 8.3% or less; Type 2 flares the apron, with the flare dimensioned at 5 ft and 2 ft 6 in on the sheet; both have a 1/2 in lip between roadway gutter and curb, and a dome panel only if the driveway is controlled by stop, yield or a signal [29]. The sheet says to avoid placing drainage structures or junction boxes in front of a driveway entrance [29]. By statute a car may not stand or park in front of the driveway or within 5 ft (1.5 m) of the end of the curb radius leading to it [22]. Alley widths and apron depths were not found in a source that could be read.

**The dead end.** The federal manual lets a DEAD END sign mark the entrance to a single street that ends without meeting another, a NO OUTLET sign mark a network with no other exit, and a STREET ENDS XX FT sign warn on the approach where the end is not apparent [30]. At the end itself, if an object marker is used it shall be a Type 4: a diamond at least 18 in (0.46 m) on a side, red or black with nine red reflectors each at least 3 in across, or all red, mounted at least 4 ft (1.2 m) above the edge of the traveled way [30]. A barricade marking the end shall meet the Type 3 barricade design except that its stripes are retroreflective white and red [24]. For the turnaround, Bremerton requires a cul-de-sac at every permanent street end and at a temporary dead end longer than 200 ft, and allows a hammerhead on a temporary dead end shorter than that [5]. The Seattle Fire Code's Appendix D, a model-code appendix that applies to fire access roads other than public streets and binds only where adopted, requires a turnaround on a dead end over 150 ft (46 m): for 151 to 500 ft a 120 ft hammerhead, a 60 ft Y or a 96 ft diameter cul-de-sac [31]. No cul-de-sac radius from a small Washington city's own standard could be read.

## 6. Where a town street meets a state highway, gravel or a railroad, someone else's rules govern the edge

**The state highway.** In many small towns the main street is a state route. Washington's law splits it: the city has responsibility and control beyond the curbs, but in a city or town under a population threshold the state installs, operates and maintains the traffic control devices, striping and channelization on, entering or leaving a state highway [32]. The statute sets the threshold at 27,500 and raises it in steps; for cities with 30,000 or fewer people on Jan 1, 2025 it is 32,500 from Jul 1, 2025 [32]. So at a small town's highway corner the stripes and signs are WSDOT's, and the side street is the natural minor road, since the first criterion in the manual's guidance is a roadway intersecting a designated through or numbered highway [24].

The geometry is WSDOT's too. Lanes on a state highway at 35 mph or less are 10 to 12 ft (3.0 to 3.7 m) wide [33]; the figure draws 11 ft. The corner radii are the larger ones of section 1, 30, 50 or 55 ft depending on the design vehicle [3]. For a new intersection on an existing highway the sight triangle is set back 18 ft (5.5 m) from the edge of the traveled way, the 10 ft a stopped car sits back plus 8 ft from the front of the car to the driver's eye; at an existing intersection with obstructions that cannot be removed it may be reduced to 10 ft with a written analysis [3]. If a left-turn lane is provided, WSDOT's minimum storage is 100 ft (30 m) [3]. Crosswalks across the highway are normally on all legs, marked or not, with marking set by the Region Traffic Engineer [11]. The highway's centre line, if extended, is dotted through the side street's mouth [17].

**Where the pavement ends.** The manual's guidance is a PAVEMENT ENDS sign where a paved surface changes to gravel-treated or earth; it may carry an advisory speed plaque [30]. Its size is 36 by 36 in (0.91 m) on a conventional road, with 30 by 30 in as the minimum for a low-volume road [30]. A NO CENTER LINE sign may warn of a roadway without a centre line [30]. The text gives no advance distance for the PAVEMENT ENDS sign, and no source read here describes the edge of the pavement or the shoulder where gravel starts.

**The railroad.** A street can cross a track at grade. The federal manual requires at least one Crossbuck sign, retroreflective white, on each highway approach, on the right-hand side, with an additional one on the left where sight is restricted; its minimum size is 48 by 9 in per blade [34]. Pavement markings (an X, the letters RR and transverse lines) shall be placed on paved approaches where signals or gates are, and at other crossings where the posted or statutory speed is 40 mph or more; they need not be placed below 40 mph, or in urban areas, if a diagnostic team finds other devices suitable [34]. Where a STOP sign is used with the Crossbuck a stop line should be installed, and the manual's figure shows it at least 15 ft (4.6 m) from the nearest rail and about 8 ft upstream of a gate [34]. By statute a driver stops within 50 ft but not less than 15 ft from the nearest rail when a signal, a lowered gate or a train in hazardous proximity demands it, and a car may not park, other than temporarily to load or unload, within 50 ft (15.2 m) of the nearest rail [35], [22].

## 7. Older corners differ by what the law did not ask for, and the sources on them are thin

A curb poured before the law asked for ramps has none. Washington's statute on curb ramps lists 1973 as its first session law, with changes in 1977, 1989 and 2020 [36]. Its standard is no fewer than two ramps per lineal block on or near the crosswalks at intersections, each at least 36 in wide, where curbs and sidewalks are constructed. It does not apply to a curb existing when the section was enacted, but to new curb and to replacement curb. From Sep 21, 1977 each ramp built must be matched at the other end of its crosswalk, unless there is no curb or sidewalk there [36]. The statute's 36 in is narrower than the 4 ft (48 in) WSDOT and PROWAG require [36], [11], [12]. A corner that was never rebuilt may therefore have none, or one that would not meet the standards in section 2; the statute does not say how many do.

Some old fixtures may stay. The manual allows historic street name signs in locally identified historic districts, on lower-speed roads, to stay in service without complying with several of the section's provisions [26].

That is all the sources read here support. They give no measured corner radius, crown, cross-gutter or curb material for a pre-war Washington town, and a sentence about what a 1920s corner "looked like" would be a guess, so there is none here.

## 8. To build one, draw the parts in this order, and treat the gaps as gaps

This research was done for Cape Sorrow, a game in development as of Nov 22, 2026 at [games.csarko.sh/capesorrow](https://games.csarko.sh/capesorrow).

**The order to draw them.** First the two centre lines and the angle between them. Then the pavement edges and curb lines; WSDOT's lanes on a state highway are 10 to 12 ft, and no source read here gives a width for a local street. Then the curb returns, with the radius the street kinds set. Then the verge and sidewalk behind the arc, 3 to 4 ft and 5 to 6 ft. Then, for each crossing, a ramp, its landing and its dome panel, inside the crosswalk lines. Then the crosswalks, a stop line 4 ft before each on the minor street, and the centre line with its dotted extension. Then the signs, and the curbs where parking is held back. Then whatever is special: the T, the skew, the alley, the end, the highway, the gravel and the track.

**What this research could not find in a readable source.** These are gaps, not rules.

- How the crowns of two crossing streets are blended, and any cross-gutter or valley-gutter dimension in a Washington town.
- The width of a town street's gutter pan. WSDOT's curb-and-gutter section is 1 ft 6 in wide overall [6], but the published sheet is cropped, and no city drawing was read.
- Where a storm inlet sits relative to a crosswalk. WSDOT's exhibit shows the position and gives no distance [11].
- Measured corner radii for pre-war Washington towns, and anything on stamped sidewalks, hitching rings or granite curbs.
- How far short of the crosswalk a centre line stops.
- Alley widths and apron depths, and cul-de-sac radii in a small Washington city's own standard.
- The advance distance for a PAVEMENT ENDS sign, the rails of a Type 3 barricade, and the size and spacing of word and arrow markings on the pavement.
- The width of a rail crossing's surface, and of its panels.
- Pavement widths and cross sections of small-town residential streets: no city standard read here gave one.
