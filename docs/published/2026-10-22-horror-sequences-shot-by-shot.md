# 23 Horror Sequences Broken Down Shot by Shot

**Question:** How are the most studied set pieces in horror cinema actually built, shot by shot, and what does each one show about how a director, cinematographer and editor move an audience from calm to dread to shock and back?

**Short answer:** Across 23 sequences from Psycho (1960) to Weapons (2025), the same construction recurs under very different surfaces. A long, quiet lead-in gives the audience something to watch (a drain, a cigarette, a children's song, a spoon in a teacup) and usually gives it more knowledge than the character on screen; the camera then holds a wide or deep frame long enough for the viewer to find the threat first, or follows a character so smoothly that the move itself becomes the menace. The release is short and rationed: a sub-second cut to a close-up on a loud sound, a change of scale from very wide to very close, or a hard cut from a long moving shot to something motionless. Shock is assembled in the cut rather than shown (the knife and the body in separate shots, the decapitation heard and not seen, the hammer blow behind a sliding door), and the aftermath is held long enough to let the audience absorb it. Sound carries what the frame withholds: footsteps that stop, a bus's air brakes, a tricycle crossing from carpet to parquet, thunder matched to each room. Where a production account exists, these choices are documented decisions, not accidents, and several were made against the wishes of producers, composers or the first cut. The principles these sequences illustrate, the directors' stated intentions and the evidence for what works are set out on the companion page, [How Horror Directors Build Dread Shot by Shot](/research/horror-cinematography-shot-by-shot).

## 1. How to read the breakdowns

Each breakdown has three parts. **Documented** collects what can be sourced: a production account, storyboard, filmmaker or editor interview, or a published shot analysis, with the disputes between sources flagged where they exist. **Reconstruction** is a beat-by-beat account of the sequence as it plays, with approximate shot sizes and durations that have not been checked against a timed viewing; treat every duration in a reconstruction as an estimate, and treat any beat marked unverified as a viewer's recollection rather than a fact. **What it teaches** states the transferable craft point in one or two sentences. The sequences are grouped by era: the classic set pieces from Hollywood and Britain, the Japanese and Korean sequences, and the modern ones from 2013 onwards. The closing section lists the facts about these sequences that remain disputed or unconfirmed.

## 2. Classic sequences: Hollywood and Britain, 1942-1980

### 2.1 Psycho (1960): the shower scene

**Documented.** The scene was shot December 17-23, 1959, and runs about three minutes; the shot counts are disputed in print: Hitchcock told Truffaut "seventy camera setups for forty-five seconds of footage" and elsewhere "seventy-eight pieces of the film," Philip Skerry counted 60 shots, and the 2017 documentary 78/52 takes its title from 78 setups and 52 cuts [1], [2]. Black and white was chosen partly "to keep the shower scene from being too gory"; the final shot "starts with an extreme close-up on Marion's eye and zooms in and out," and the water made Janet Leigh want to blink [3]. Hitchcock originally wanted no music; Bernard Herrmann insisted on his cue, strings play muted everywhere except here, and Hitchcock later said "33% of the effect of Psycho was due to the music" [1]. The common claim that the knife never touches flesh is contradicted by a frame analysis finding one shot where it appears to enter the abdomen [1]. Saul Bass's storyboards are "similar, though not exactly the same" as the film (the shower-head motif is not in them); Bass later claimed to have directed the scene, Leigh ("he never directed me. Absolutely not") and assistant director Hilton Green denied it, and the side-by-side comparison shows Hitchcock did not simply shoot Bass's boards [4], [5], [6].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Marion at the desk totals the money, tears up the sheet, flushes it: medium shots, the flush is the first drain. (2) She undresses and steps in; shots of the shower head from below and the side; her face in relief, eyes closed; about 50-60 seconds of calm. (3) Behind the translucent curtain, screen left, a figure enters and approaches, visible to the audience and not to Marion; this one "audience knows more" beat lasts seconds. (4) The curtain is torn open, the silhouette raises the knife, the unmuted strings begin; the montage runs roughly 20-25 seconds of very short shots alternating knife arm, face, mouth, torso, feet and attacker, with knife and body kept in separate shots so the violence is assembled in the cut. (5) The attacker leaves; slower shots resume; her hand slides down the tiles; the curtain rings pop one by one; she falls over the rim. (6) Blood swirls into the drain; a slow dissolve from drain to open eye; the camera pulls back and levels her face, tracks across the room to the newspaper hiding the money and to the window, from which Norman's cry is heard.

**What it teaches.** The lead-in and the coda are long, slow and centred on a single object; the murder is the only passage in which the eye is denied a fixed point, which is why it reads as more violent than anything shown. The pull-back converts the audience from Marion's accomplice to Norman's.

### 2.2 Psycho (1960): the Arbogast staircase murder

**Documented.** Screenwriter Joseph Stefano moved the death from the foyer to the staircase to hide the truth about Mother "without signaling that something was hidden"; the overhead angle was "necessary to hide the film's twist," and a pulley-mounted dolly track and chair rig were built and tested over several weeks [3]. Hitchcock scrapped the stair-climb inserts Hilton Green had directed from Bass's boards because they showed "not an innocent person but a sinister man" [3]; Bass's inserts of hands gripping the railing and feet on each step were "exactly wrong" because they signalled danger before Arbogast reached the top [6]. The fall was a rear-projected background plate with Martin Balsam flailing in a chair, so the background looks soft against the sharp actor [7]. Before the climb Hitchcock cuts to a door opening a crack at the top, and the level following shot then "leaps to a ceiling view" in which the figures look small [8].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Arbogast enters the dark house, looks about the hall; the audience has been told Mother is upstairs. (2) The upstairs door opens a crack: the audience now knows more than the detective. (3) A slow, level following shot of the climb with no inserts. (4) At the landing, the cut to the extreme overhead; Mother rushes out as a small flat figure, face hidden. (5) A large close-up of the slashed face; the jump from very wide to very close is the shock. (6) The rear-projected fall, arms wheeling. (7) Mother on top of him, two stabs, cut. Roughly 60-90 seconds from door to cut.

**What it teaches.** Coverage changes meaning: close-ups of feet and hands on a staircase code the climber as a predator. The overhead solves a plot problem (hide the face) and an emotional one (make the attacker's entrance a change of scale rather than of place) in one shot.

### 2.3 The Birds (1963): the schoolhouse jungle gym

**Documented.** Hitchcock said "the pattern of The Birds was deliberately to go slow"; the film has no conventional score, only sound effects, sparse source music and "calculated silences," with electronic bird noises by Oskar Sala and Remi Gassmann [9]. A few crows land unnoticed; Hitchcock cuts to Melanie smoking in close-ups showing impatience; "each cut back is a wider shot showing more birds on the perch"; she follows one bird overhead to the swarmed playground while the children sing a nonsense song throughout [10]. "The full mass of birds appears only at the end of the sequence, when Melanie turns around" [11]. Whether the jungle-gym crows were mostly real or mostly cut-outs is disputed between sources [9], [12].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Melanie arrives; the children's round is already audible. (2) She sits on the bench facing camera, the jungle gym behind and to her right. (3) Jungle gym: one crow. (4) Melanie lights a cigarette. (5) Three or four crows. (6) Medium close-up of Melanie. (7) A dozen or more. (8) A long single close-up of her smoking, the longest shot of the sequence; the song's repetition is the clock. (9) A crow flies overhead; her eyes track it. (10) The pan follows the bird to the covered frame. (11) She rises and backs to the door, the song still going. About three minutes from bench to attack.

**What it teaches.** The crows multiply only across cuts, never inside a shot, so the viewer is forced to count; the revealing pan is motivated by the character's eyeline, so the audience's superior knowledge and her discovery fuse in one move. It is the bomb under the table in its purest staging.

### 2.4 Cat People (1942): the walk and the bus; the pool

**Documented.** Alice walks alone at night, stalked by Irena; the film cuts to close-ups as her face shifts from cheerful to frightened and she quickens; the footsteps stop; a bus's air brakes hiss. Editor Mark Robson called it an "editing device [he] created by accident" and added a loud air-brake sound that "knocked viewers out of their seats"; the unit put a "bus" in every subsequent film [13]. The track is nearly silent apart from Alice's footsteps; Irena's "faster, louder heels" alert her; a growl builds into a hiss revealed as the bus, which enters screen right while Alice looks screen left [14]. A shot-by-shot essay notes the lamppost moving from frame right to centre to left across the shots as a continuity marker and a J-cut into the wide as the bus roars in [15]. In the pool scene the lights go out, growls circle, and the big-cat shadow on the wall was a backlit fist [16], [17]. Credit for the device is itself disputed: the Jump Scare encyclopaedia entry names Tourneur, the Cat People entry and several critics name Robson, and Lewton is the usual popular attribution [18], [13].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Alice leaves the restaurant and walks along the Central Park wall; Irena follows at a distance. (2) Alternating shots: Alice past the lampposts, medium-wide, tracking; Irena's heels on the pavement, close, tracking; two sets of heel-clicks as the rhythm. (3) Alice quickens; the follower's clicks stop. (4) She stops, turns, sees nothing. (5) A growl, a swaying branch. (6) The bus slams in from the right on a wide with its brake hiss, the one hard cut. (7) She boards. (8) The zoo keeper finds slaughtered sheep and paw prints that become a woman's shoe prints. About two minutes for the walk.

**What it teaches.** The false release spends the accumulated tension on a harmless object, and the sheep immediately afterwards tell the audience the danger was real. The device's anatomy was complete at birth (quiet build, footstep-scale sounds, entry from the side the character is not looking, loud sting, false payoff); everything since is dosage.

### 2.5 Halloween (1978): the Panaglide opening and the Shape in the background

**Documented.** The opening is a roughly four- to five-minute take from the killer's point of view, shot on the final day of the 20-day schedule over about 16 hours, with three operators rotating because nobody could wear the 70-pound Panaglide for long and crew moving lights out of frame as the camera passed; "It seems like one take, but there are a couple of hidden cuts" [19], [20]. Cundey: Carpenter "decided that the opening shot should be the point of view of someone mysterious," and the stabilised rig became "the signature movement for Michael Myers" [21], [22]. Redfern's shot data gives the opening as four shots of 8.3, 8.3, 258.9 and 31.5 seconds [23]. Critics describe the later daytime appearances as deep staging in the anamorphic frame with Michael far back, still and slightly soft, "making the audience see the threat first" [24]; Cundey himself names the "lurking space" at the edges of the anamorphic frame "where something might or might not be hiding" [25].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Approach from the street to the lit house and the porch pumpkin. (2) The side window: Judith and her boyfriend on the couch, then upstairs. (3) Wait; the bedroom light goes out. (4) Around the back, through the kitchen door; a hand takes a knife from a drawer, the first time the camera has a body. (5) The boyfriend leaves. (6) Up the stairs; a clown mask is lifted and put on; the frame becomes a two-eyehole matte (a hidden cut almost certainly sits here). (7) Judith at the mirror turns ("Michael!"); the stabbing, the knife entering from the frame edge, the matte jerking. (8) Down and out the front door. (9) A car pulls up; the mask is pulled off. (10) The reverse: a child in a clown suit with a bloody knife; the crane rises and pulls back as the theme returns. The later appearances (across the street by the station wagon, behind the hedge, outside the classroom, among the laundry) each follow the same shape: a static wide, the Shape in deep background, and an absence on the second look.

**What it teaches.** The matte literalises seeing through a mask and lets the hidden cut pass; the reveal rereads the camera's height and clumsiness. The repeated absence on the second look trains the audience to disbelieve Laurie along with everyone else, which is tension released without a scare.

### 2.6 The Shining (1980): the Big Wheel, Room 237 and the maze

**Documented.** Garrett Brown tried following Danny on foot, was "too winded after an entire three-minute take," and mounted the Steadicam arm on a modified wheelchair that took the lens to about an inch off the floor; at least thirty takes, each several minutes; "no one anticipated how amazing the sound of the Big Wheel running alternately over the bare floor and carpet would be" [26], [27]. The maze was shot entirely on Steadicam with a 9.8 mm Kinoptik at about 24 inches of lens height, mostly fluid moves ahead of or behind Wendy and Danny; Danny's feet were shot three inches off the deck and Brown stilt-walked backwards in Danny's footprints [26]. Kubrick wanted the Steadicam "as a tool which can help get the lens where it's wanted in space and time," demanded two or three perfect takes a scene, and chose on performance [28]. John Alcott lit the hotel almost entirely by in-shot practicals because the 360-degree camera ruled out floor and grid lights, with an 860-lamp daylight backing behind the lounge windows [29].

**Reconstruction.** Big Wheel: a single low following shot behind Danny, the sound alternating as he crosses rugs and parquet; he brakes at 237, tries the handle, a flash of the twins' bodies, the ball; the ride resumes. Second ride: he turns a corner and the cut is to a static wide of the twins hand in hand at the corridor's end, intercut with flash frames of their bodies and the axe; he covers his eyes; they are gone. Room 237: Jack's moving POV through the suite to the bath; the kiss framed in the mirror, in which the audience sees the decayed body before Jack turns. Maze: the daylight walk cut against Jack over the model, the slow move down into the model until the real figures seem to appear in it, the night chase on the 24-inch following shots and the 3-inch feet.

**What it teaches.** The pattern is long moving shot, then sudden static shot: the camera stopping is the scare. The lens height and the sound do the tension work; the editorial release is always a hard cut to something motionless, and the mirror is the device for letting the audience know first.

### 2.7 Alien (1979): the chestburster

**Documented.** Scott ran "four or five cameras" because the blood would spray the white set and there could be no second day; he kept the creature from the actors ("I never wanted them to see it"); on the first run the T-shirt did not open, so he cleared the set, climbed onto Hurt and razor-bladed the shirt, and the second run was "perfect," so the popular "one take" story is half true [30], [31]. Kubrick phoned to say "I've run it through slowly, I can't see the cut" [31]. Editor Terry Rawlings cut the film "deliberately slowly to build suspense" so the tense moments land [32].

**Reconstruction.** (1) The crew eating and joking in warm light, the longest stretch of ordinary behaviour in the film. (2) Kane coughs, chokes, is laid on the table; concern turns to alarm in medium shots. (3) Convulsions, the group holding him; because the coverage is simultaneous, the cuts are between angles of one continuous event. (4) The shirt bulges, blood blooms, the creature breaks out in a few seconds. (5) Reaction close-ups. (6) The creature screeches off the table and out of frame; silence. About two minutes from first cough to exit. The air-shaft hunt (Dallas with the flamethrower, Lambert on the tracker, the blip approaching, the arms rising into frame, the dropped flamethrower spinning) is reconstruction only; no production account was found.

**What it teaches.** The audience knows exactly as much as the crew; this is the film's one pure surprise, placed after a long lull, and the multi-camera method is what makes the cut invisible because there is no reconstructed continuity, only selection.

### 2.8 Jaws (1975): the Alex Kintner beach attack

**Documented.** Shot over three days on State Beach in June 1974; a compressed-air blood cylinder about 30 yards offshore; divers pulled the boy under; retakes waited an hour for the blood to wash away; the mechanical shark biting a mannequin was second unit [33]. The first full view of the shark is a glimpse so brief viewers forget it, and the sequence relies on "expert blocking within roaming long takes," the camera behind objects, Verna Fields cutting oblivious beachgoers against the water (critic's reading) [34]. The dolly zoom on Brody is an homage to Vertigo [35], [36]. Spielberg on the broken shark: "The shark not working was a godsend. It made me become more like Alfred Hitchcock than like Ray Harryhausen" [37].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Wide beach; Brody in a deck chair in profile. (2) A heavy man floats: false alarm. (3) A girl screams; her boyfriend has lifted her: false alarm. (4) A black bathing cap submerges and resurfaces: false alarm. (5) Between these, townspeople cross the lens and a man leans in about parking, blocking Brody's view; the camera holds on him straining to see past. (6) Alex asks for ten more minutes and paddles out on the yellow raft. (7) A dog's stick floats alone. (8) Underwater, legs kicking, the camera rising toward the raft. (9) The attack: a fountain of blood, the raft flipping, the glimpse of the shark, screams. (10) The dolly zoom on the seated Brody as the beach erupts. (11) He runs everyone out. (12) The torn raft washes in; Mrs Kintner calls her son. About four minutes.

**What it teaches.** The false alarms teach the audience to distrust its own alarm so the real attack lands as it relaxes; the obstructions make the audience want what Brody wants, a clear view, which is why the subjective lurch of the dolly zoom works.

### 2.9 The Exorcist (1973): the bedroom escalation

**Documented.** Owen Roizman rebuilt the bedroom inside a refrigerated enclosure held near 20 below zero so breath would show (at 25 degrees there was too little), backlit the breath, and made the room monochrome (gray-taupe walls, beige sheets, black clothing, no white) "so skin tones became the only real color there" [38]. The room was lit with hidden inkies on dimmers that flicker and misbehave under the demon's influence; when the room shakes a lamp falls and stays on the floor, "which changes the room's lighting for the rest of the sequence"; the ending used shadowless bounce for "an ethereal, soft, cool glow" [38]. The doctors' arrival is one unbroken hoist-and-chair move up the winding stairs into the room [38]. Friedkin: "The pacing is deliberate"; every light "had a visible, realistic source"; the two-to-three-frame demon flashes were added on the Moviola "to create, achieve, and sustain a kind of dreamlike state" and "were not planned originally" [39], [40], [41]. How many flashes there are is disputed between sources [41].

**Reconstruction (the doctors' visit).** The one-take stair climb carries the audience into the room as a witness; inside, static medium shots of Regan thrashing, the bed bucking, the doctors restraining her; backlit breath; the slap; the voice change; Chris in the doorway.

**What it teaches.** The possession is legible as a lighting arc before any effect appears: practicals flicker when the demon acts, the fallen lamp overturns the room's geometry of shadow, and the finale is shadowless and cool, all of it from diegetic sources. The hoist shot denies the audience the relief of a cut on the way in.

### 2.10 The Texas Chain Saw Massacre (1974): the dinner and the first kill

**Documented.** The dinner ran more than 26 hours straight in a blacked-out room above 100 degrees with formaldehyde-preserved props rotting; it was shot mostly in order, reframing rather than stopping; Hooper wanted multiple masters from each end of the table and close-ups "simulating point-of-view shots," and Gunnar Hansen lunged down the table at Marilyn Burns while Lou Perryman pulled focus [42]. Daniel Pearl shot on an Éclair NPR with ISO 25 reversal stock, one dolly and 60 feet of track, keeping shots "moving with a point"; minimal light "gave a bit of a documentary feeling"; the swing shot was an 8 mm Zeiss hung off a low dolly pushed under the swing so "the house will start out small in the frame, and as we come closer, it will grow and grow" [43]. Hooper confirms the documentary aim and the near-bloodless design [44], [45]. The door slam is described only by critics: Kirk is struck and dragged into a back room, "A metal door slams shut with a heavy, booming sound," and the meat hook is never shown penetrating [46].

**Reconstruction.** Dinner: wide masters from each end establish the tableau (Sally tied in the armchair, the family either side, Grandpa at the head); the film tightens through medium shots of the taunting and Leatherface's lunges to the series of extreme close-ups of Sally's eyeball and the skin around it, cut faster against the laughter; then Grandpa over the bucket, the hammer dropped repeatedly, cuts between hands, hammer and face. First kill: Kirk on the porch; the hallway through the open door, the red wall and skulls at its end; he trips on the threshold; Leatherface fills the doorway from the side; one hammer blow; the legs spasm; a second blow; he is dragged in and the metal door slides shut. Perhaps 15 seconds.

**What it teaches.** The dinner has no suspense gap left to exploit (Sally and the audience know everything), so Hooper substitutes duration and proximity, an endurance structure rather than a reveal structure; the eye close-ups are POV-simulating shots turned inside out, not what she sees but the organ of seeing. The door slam lets the viewer finish the act, which is how a nearly bloodless film became remembered as the most violent of its decade.

## 3. Japanese and Korean sequences, 1998-2016

### 3.1 Ringu (1998): Sadako comes through the television

**Documented.** Nakata kept the film free of on-screen deaths over producers' doubts, showed Sadako as a character "who shows only one eye and her hair," and modelled his restraint on Robert Wise's The Haunting, which he reportedly watched at least five times [47], [48]. The cursed-video footage was shot on 35 mm with a lab-added grain, and the sound team mixed four telephone qualities so the phones "did not sound like Hollywood phones" [47]. Cinematographer Junichiro Hayashi insisted at the 2019 restoration that the first grade "was too warm, and needed to be cooler, as well as darker," and that it still "hadn't gone dark enough" [49]. The often repeated account that Rie Inō was filmed walking backwards and the footage reversed appears only in secondary sources and is unconfirmed by any filmmaker statement [50], [51].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Ryuji alone at his desk in daylight, believing the curse lifted; static medium shot. (2) The TV switches itself on: static, then the well in its clearing, the grainy monochrome frame-within-a-frame the audience recognises before Ryuji reacts. (3) Inside the TV image a white-clad figure climbs from the well and advances toward the lens in halting motion; a long, nearly unbroken take with cutaways only to Ryuji's face. (4) She reaches the plane of the screen and comes through it: hands, then body; an insert of torn fingernails as she crawls. (5) Ryuji retreats; medium shots alternate with Sadako rising, hair over face; a drone, no music cue until late. (6) The hair parts; an extreme close-up of one bulging eye; cut to Ryuji's frozen, distorted face, the death mask seen on earlier victims. (7) The phone rings; Reiko hears only breathing; the room is shown empty, the TV on static.

**What it teaches.** The scene is built as shared discovery (audience and character know the same thing), which is why it holds one space with minimal cutting; the threshold does the work a monster's attack does in Western horror.

### 3.2 Audition (1999): the sack; the final scene

**Documented.** Miike: "The movie continues for about 50 minutes, betraying the delusions and expectations of the audience"; in the second half "the scenes are seamlessly connected, making it appear almost like one scene," so the audience "cannot predict events" [52]. On what is shown: Shiina "smiles when she cuts off the foot, and as a result that moment becomes real horror. If another actress would have done it very seriously and roughly, then it wouldn't become horror" [53]. Critics describe Asami "waiting by the phone in her empty apartment" while "a large tied sack" sits in the background and lurches when the phone rings [54], [55].

**Reconstruction.** The sack: one static wide of a bare room, Asami seated by the telephone, head bowed, hair curtaining her face, the sack against the rear wall; the phone rings and rings and nothing moves for well over a minute, long enough for the eye to settle on the sack; her lips lift into a smile; the sack convulses with a guttural sound; a hard cut back to the romance, which does not return to the sack for about 45 minutes. The finale: a hallucination montage knotting memory and fantasy; a floor-level shot of the paralysed Aoyama with Asami above him, cheerful, in apron and gloves; close-ups of the syringe and the needles with her soft "kiri kiri kiri" in place of score; the piano wire around the ankle, with the camera on her absorbed face and his rather than the wound; the son's key in the door; a cut to the Izu hotel bed as if it were all a dream; a cut back to the floor.

**What it teaches.** The sack is the film's first formal rupture, a static tableau in a film cut like television drama, and it hands the audience information the protagonist will not get for 45 minutes. The torture holds on faces because Miike is building an intimacy, not a gore set piece.

### 3.3 The Wailing (2016): the cross-cut rituals

**Documented.** The press kit states that the shamanic ritual "was shot in a 15-minute long take using 6 different cameras," that Hong Kyung-pyo made "selective use of an anamorphic lens in order to create changes in the depiction of certain spaces," and that editor Kim Sun-min "allows the tension in THE WAILING to build at a very slow pace," withholding resolution "for longer and longer stretches as the film progresses" [56], [57]. Na: "The music used in the sequence is played by actual shamans" and the actions match a real exorcism; he "disregarded my storyboard after seeing him search through the woods," and the two "shot it like jazz," a phrase Hong uses independently [58], [59]. Critics describe the shaman's flame-and-blood kut cross-cut with the Japanese stranger's inward ceremony in "the sort of dazzling rhythmic effect that Na and editor Kim Sun-min pull off" [60], [61]. No statement from Na, Hong or Kim about why the rituals are cross-cut was found.

**Reconstruction.** (1) Night courtyard: Il-gwang in robes, drummers and gong, burning paper totems, a tethered goat; wide anamorphic frames hold the whole yard. (2) Inside, Hyo-jin restrained on the floor, screaming; handheld, close. (3) The dance accelerates with the drums; knives, stakes, the goat killed; the six-camera coverage lets the cutting ride the drum tempo without breaking a continuous performance. (4) Cross-cut: the Stranger alone with a candle, a hand drum and photographs, hammering a stake; tighter, dimmer, his drum against theirs; the units shorten. (5) Each stake, a harder convulsion, Jong-gu's face between. (6) Jong-gu overturns the altar; the drums cut out; the Stranger stops as if he heard; the girl goes still.

**What it teaches.** The audience sees both rituals, the father one; the editing makes the viewer certain of a causal reading (stranger evil, shaman cure) that the last act inverts, which is Na's stated "give up 30 to gain 70" ambiguity and the editor's brief to withhold resolution [58].

### 3.4 A Tale of Two Sisters (2003) and I Saw the Devil (2010): the sink, the taxi

**Documented.** Kim Jee-woon's production diary records overnight wallpaper and fabric tests, a decision that the "right colour and tone" could be reached in camera without digital grading, ambient lighting that took more than twice as long as normal, and a near-360-degree dolly move for Su-mi's descent; his stated meaning is that the "strong, colorful designs and patterns" are repressed memories that "will come up and torture you," with the scare "executed at a very slow tempo" [62], [63], [64]. A critic notes the camera takes a position under the kitchen sink "acting as a ghost under the kitchen sink without revealing the ghost" [65]. For the taxi, Choi Min-sik: "we took the body of a taxicab, cut off the top of it, replaced it with panels that were acrylic," spun it physically in a studio with "the camera moving back and forth out of frame," and composited the exteriors [66].

**Reconstruction.** Dinner: symmetrical static framing in the red room; glances; cuts to characters not present; Mi-hee chokes and falls; from her floor-level view, a pale, bluish girl crouched in the gap under the sink turns her head with a jerk, then is gone, the only cool colour in a red room. Taxi: an interior two-shot; the camera begins to orbit; two stabbings as the car veers and blood hits the acrylic; the orbit continues as if unbroken over a composited road.

**What it teaches.** The sink ghost is granted to an outsider, which the twist later makes the film's one objective apparition; the taxi orbit denies the viewer any exit and tells the audience the hunted man is more dangerous than his hunters.

## 4. Modern sequences, 2013-2025

### 4.1 Get Out (2017): the tea and the Sunken Place

**Documented.** Toby Oliver shot the whole film on four Angenieux zooms because a first-time director found "infinite variability" easier than primes; the signature close-ups are a wide lens pushed to "literally inches away from her face," which "puts the audience in a slightly uncomfortable position," and he insists such moves be "fully motivated and justified by what's happening in the story" [67], [68]. The estate's warmth was "a bit of a visual false security"; Chris's night walk is two long Steadicam takes [69], [67]. The Sunken Place was Kaluuya on a wire in a black-draped space, the camera moving around him, shot "dry for wet" at about 200 fps with a single bright light standing for the screen above, modelled on Under the Skin [69]. Peele: "You know when you're about to fall asleep and you feel like you're falling, but you catch yourself and you wake up? Well, what if you never caught yourself?"; the place "is a metaphor for the marginalization of the Black horror movie audience" [70].

**Reconstruction.** (1) Night; Chris slips out to smoke; Walter sprints at him and veers; Georgina fixes her reflection at a lit window; cyan-green night light. (2) Missy's lamp is on; "Come sit with me"; conventional two-shot and shot-reverse at medium. (3) The hypnotherapy offer, the mother; an insert of the spoon circling the cup, its clink the metronome; coverage tightens by increments, the zooms allowing it without resetting. (4) The memory of the television and the dying mother; an insert of fingernails scratching the chair arm; the frame now very close. (5) "Sink into the floor." (6) The Sunken Place: slow fall through black, particles drifting, a small bright rectangle above with Missy tiny and muffled. (7) Hard cut: morning.

**What it teaches.** The creeping closeness is justified diegetically by hypnosis, which is why it is the film's most sustained tightening before the games room; the spoon and the fingernails are the two objects the eye is sent to, and both return as the plot's hinges.

### 4.2 The Witch (2015): Caleb at the hut; Thomasin's walk into the wood

**Documented.** About 85 percent of the film was shot on a single Cooke Series II 32 mm at around T2.3 ("You feel 'right there' with the characters," with bokeh "like looking through a porthole or crystal ball"); the Alexa was framed 1.66:1 because the house needed to feel intimate while the trees needed to look tall; day interiors were lit only by shaped daylight, night interiors by practical and off-camera flame, with the one stage-built moon kept well below the lantern level; the grade was "gray and dead" [71]. Eggers: "I think the audience's imagination is much more powerful than anything I can give them," and he would cut Alien's last shot of the creature because it looks like "a guy in a suit" [72]. The devil's offers (butter, a pretty dress, seeing the world) were kept because "Those were things the devil actually promised" [73]. Neither Eggers nor Blaschke has described the hut seduction or the sabbath shot by shot in any reachable source; a claim that butoh dancers helped choreograph the coven appears in a single review and is unverified [74].

**Reconstruction.** Hut: grey overcast wood on the 32 mm, trunks filling the frame's height; the dog opened on the forest floor as a short flat insert; a wide of a stick-and-moss hovel with a dark door; Caleb's approach in one slow move with the eye on the doorway; a young woman in a red cloak steps out and advances; cuts between faces; she bends to kiss him and an aged hand closes on the back of his head; cut. The interior is never shown. Final walk: long static holds on the blood-soaked girl at dusk; the lantern-lit goat shed, a man's voice from the dark, a boot, a hand, a shoulder, "Wouldst thou like to live deliciously?"; the walk into near-black trees; firelight as the only key on the ring of women; they rise; she rises; her face passes from fear to laughter as the shot tilts up past the branches.

**What it teaches.** The audience saw the hag in the prologue, so the red cloak is staged as a fairy-tale lure and the one revealing detail is withheld to a hand. The single lens and the tall frame make the hovel and the clearing small and the trees tall, and the "porthole" bokeh isolates one face at a time.

### 4.3 It Follows (2014): the opening street shot and the hallway pan

**Documented.** The opening is "a 360 degree pan with some zooms on about 50ft of track"; with no remote head, Gioulakis operated from the dolly in a circle while grips kept pace, and there were "only a couple of good takes" before the light went [75]. Mitchell wanted "very deep focus" and scenes staged "as a tableau," "a Rear Window-style approach" that uses depth of field to build suspense "instead of hiding threats for jump scares," with minimal coverage, single-take masters, a "distant, sterile" camera and handheld only for Jay's subjective passages [75]. Roughly 80 percent of the film is on the 18 mm [76]. The hallway shot is "a slow, 720-degree pan around the hallway" through a window and back; whether the follower appears in it is disputed: one review states "nothing appears in the frame" [77].

**Reconstruction.** Opening: a girl in heels and underwear runs from a house; the camera on its track pans with her in a full circle as she crosses the street, hesitates, is asked if she is all right, and drives off; cut to the beach and the morning body. Hallway: Jay in the school office; a slow rotation through the window to the corridor and grounds; students; on the second rotation (unverified) a tall old woman in a hospital gown walking toward the camera from the far end.

**What it teaches.** The single rotation does the film's thesis: the threat is not shown, the geography is, and the audience learns to scan. The slow rotation is the suspense form; the handheld POV shots are reserved for the surprise form.

### 4.4 Hereditary (2018): the car and its aftermath

**Documented.** Aster wanted "a family tragedy that curdled into a nightmare" [78]; Pogorzelski approached the lighting "from a dramatic perspective," hardening it in the third act while keeping it "grounded in the reality" [79]. Aster handed his editors a detailed shot list on day one ("It was like he already had the whole movie edited in his head"), but single-camera-designed scenes were "difficult to rework when they didn't come together" [80]. The sound team describes the decapitation as "off-camera at that point," built from sound alone with Aster setting when to "hit bone," and the aftermath as "just so quiet. I think there is one simple cricket sound happening," held "just the right amount of time to absorb the gravity" [81]. No production account of how the scene was shot or cut was found.

**Reconstruction.** (1) The party; Charlie's throat swells; Peter bundles her into the car. (2) Interior, fast; she leans her head out of the window. (3) The dead deer; the swerve; the camera inside the car; a thud; the car stops. (4) A long static hold on Peter from the passenger side, hands on the wheel, not turning round. (5) The slow drive home, the back seat never shown. (6) He lies down. (7) Morning: the camera holds on his face in bed as, off screen, footsteps, the garage door, a pause, and the scream rising to a howl; the camera does not move. (8) Cut to the roadside, the head in the dirt, ants, sun.

**What it teaches.** The audience knows from the thud; Peter refuses to see; Annie learns last. The sequence establishes the film's rule of showing the aftermath instead of the act and then breaks it with the head.

### 4.5 Nope (2022): the Gordy's Home sequence

**Documented.** Editor Nicholas Monsour: "Somewhere around halfway through the edit, Jordan had this notion of bringing Gordy's storyline up to the beginning of the movie. It wasn't in the script that way"; the violence is modelled on how people "tune out psychologically during these traumatic experiences"; "the long disembodied POV take" withholds context so viewers may not know what they are seeing until the end; Peele was precise about how often Gordy pounds the ground; the standing shoe "keeps giving you more reads the more you look at it" and "doesn't really need explaining" [82], [83].

**Reconstruction.** Cold open: a sitcom set after the fact, balloons, a prone body, a chimp in a party hat with blood on its mouth, a standing shoe; the chimp approaches the camera; title. Second act, Jupe's memory: the taping; a balloon pops; the attack mostly off frame, blows heard; a low shot from under the tablecloth where the boy hides, the shoe upright on its heel in his eyeline and in focus, the violence beyond it soft; Gordy finds him and extends a fist; a gunshot.

**What it teaches.** Aftermath first with no context, then the event through a child's partial, fixated view; the shoe is the eye-sending device, an impossible object the camera and the child stare at instead of the violence.

### 4.6 The Conjuring (2013): hide-and-clap

**Documented.** By day, Carolyn plays the game blindfolded; clapping draws her to the wardrobe; the doors open and hands emerge to signal her; "Carolyn, blindfolded, cannot see them. The audience can." By night, the wardrobe doors rattle, Andrea throws them open on nothing but clothing, then looks up to a crouching figure on top; the framing keeps the top of the wardrobe out of view until the reveal "so the jolt is at its most potent," after the eye has been sent repeatedly to the interior [84]. The claps are heard before anything is seen and drive Carolyn through the house to a disembodied voice asking to play [85]. Wan on the sequel's one static scene: "The presence of music is very powerful, but so is the absence of sound as well" [86].

**Reconstruction (the basement).** Carolyn locked in, descending in the dark; each struck match lights a small sphere; two claps directly behind her ear; the match blows out.

**What it teaches.** The blindfold literalises "audience knows, character blind"; the same wardrobe is then used for misdirection, the eye sent inside and the scare arriving from above. In the basement the eye is given nothing at all and sound carries the scare.

### 4.7 Barbarian (2022): the two descents

**Documented.** Zach Kuperstein: "The upstairs should feel like a David Fincher movie, and the downstairs should feel like a Sam Raimi movie," controlled and deliberate above, "fast-paced, almost ridiculous" below; the tunnels were "lit only with flashlights" at ISO 2,500 on flare-free Supreme primes because Cregger said "I don't want any flares from the flashlight. That's too much" [87]. The interior was warm so it would feel "welcoming and not like you're walking into a horror movie," and the method was "keeping something bright in the background so you can make the rest of the image dark" [88].

**Reconstruction.** Tess descends for toilet paper by phone light; the door locks behind her; the rope, the hidden door; a long following shot down a narrow corridor with the frame mostly black and one moving pool of light; the room with the mattress, bucket, tripod camera and bloody handprint; she flees. Later AJ repeats the descent with a tape measure for comedy, the audience now knowing everything he does not.

**What it teaches.** The same corridor is a suspense set when the audience knows more and a surprise set when it does not; the laugh on the second descent is the release that re-arms the next scare.

### 4.8 Weapons (2025): the opening run and the per-chapter camera

**Documented.** The opening was planned from an animatic of houses cut to a George Harrison song to time each shot; about 30 houses were scouted; a second unit shot the running children in three-hour night windows [89], [90]. Cregger refused conventional coverage ("None of that. I want it to be brutal"), there are almost no establishing shots, and each chapter has its own grammar: Alden in profile with "the world passes him by," Justine "very much being preyed upon, so the camera's more paranoid," Archer "a hunter," shot "dead center" [90], [89]. Seiple calls it "a maliciousness to the camera in that it does show you information but it's not gonna help you" [91]. Editor Joe Murphy: the bedroom-ceiling shot "is maybe only 12 or 13 frames," because "giving the audience less, letting their imaginations do some work, really ups the fear factor," and the auditorium cut built crowd noise "to a crescendo right on the cut to a silent auditorium" [92].

**Reconstruction.** A child's voice-over; a succession of static or slowly moving night exteriors of ordinary houses; in each a child comes out of the front door and runs, arms straight back, in silhouette, out of frame, the shots cut to the music's phrase length; no establishing shot of the town.

**What it teaches.** Coverage as meaning: the same event is shot in a different grammar per character, and the few moves that show "more than is necessary" are reserved to signal the audience's superior knowledge.

### 4.9 The Haunting of Hill House, "Two Storms" (2018): five long takes

**Documented.** The episode runs 53:38, with about 51 minutes in five takes of 14:19, 7:25, 17:19, 6:13 and 5:31; the choreography was written into the script; the Hill House and funeral-home sets were built on adjacent stages joined by a hallway; production paused for a month of rehearsal with stand-ins; a dummy was swapped out of the casket and child actors replaced adults during a 360-degree move around Timothy Hutton; a peewee dolly's chain broke after the good take [93], [94]. Sound designer Trevor Gates: for "the rest of the 50 minutes, there is essentially no music," with "8 to 10 different layers of thunder" run through reverbs matched to each room, and a general rule, "Don't be afraid to get quiet, and to allow yourself that quietness so that you can get loud later"; when a ghost is near, "things are slowing down," a cricket or a clock changing pitch [95].

**What it teaches.** A horror long take's cost is mostly the choreography of concealment (swaps, portals, lighting cues); the move itself is the alibi that lets ghosts and time jumps happen in plain sight, and the room-specific storm bed carries what the score would otherwise do.

## 5. Disputed facts and gaps in these breakdowns

Several of the best-known production facts behind these sequences are contested in print and should be quoted with care. The Psycho shower scene's counts vary by source (70 or 78 setups; 50, 52 or 60 cuts; "forty-five seconds" for the stabbing against "some three minutes" for the scene), and its authorship is disputed between Saul Bass's later claims and the denials of Janet Leigh and Hilton Green; the claim that the knife never touches flesh is contradicted by frame analysis [1], [4], [5]. The number and placement of The Exorcist's subliminal demon frames differ between sources, and no authoritative frame log exists [41]. The Cat People bus is credited variously to Robson, Tourneur and Lewton [18], [13]. The jungle-gym crows in The Birds are described both as mostly real and as mostly cut-outs [9], [12]. The Alien chestburster was not a single take but two runs with several cameras [30]. Whether anything appears in It Follows' hallway pan is disputed between a published review and viewers' recollection [77]. The account that Sadako's walk was filmed backwards and reversed appears in every secondary source and in no filmmaker statement that could be reached; it should be reported as widely repeated rather than confirmed [50], [51]. Ring's budget is given as $1.5 million by one source and $1.2 million by Nakata himself [48], [96]. The Chain Saw dinner is "more than 26 hours" in the fullest account and "27 hours" in circulation [42]. Weapons' lens package is listed as Canon K-35 primes in one account and "K35 zooms" in another [89], [91].

Some of the documented material rests on secondary relays. Nakata's statement that he watched The Haunting at least five times is relayed through an encyclopaedia summary of a newspaper interview [48]. Hitchcock's own explanation of the Arbogast overhead, in the Truffaut interviews, is cited only second hand. The claim that butoh dancers helped choreograph The Witch's coven appears in a single review and is unverified [74].

The gaps are specific. No primary statement by Nakata or Hayashi on how the television scene was staged, or by Na, Hong or Kim Sun-min on why The Wailing's rituals are cross-cut, was reached; Korean- and Japanese-language cinematography press would be the next place to look. No account of how Hereditary's car scene was shot or cut, how The Witch's hut or sabbath were rigged, how Jaws' dolly zoom was executed, or how Alien's air-shaft hunt was lit was found, so those passages are reconstruction only. Flanagan's reasoning about what the Hill House long take does to a viewer, as opposed to how it was made, is not in any reachable text. Every reconstruction's shot sizes and durations await a timed viewing.
