Opening a game like a film: sixty seconds of drive, radio and dread

  • 23 min read

Question: Day Hike is a first-person co-op hiking game in the browser (Babylon.js), set on a coast and forest like Washington's Olympic Peninsula. Its opening is to be a short film: a park ranger drives the coast road alone, takes a routine call from dispatch about a hiker last seen at Trail 14, the signal breaks up on the words that matter, the ranger pulls onto the shoulder by the trailhead board, steps out and faces the trail, and the game cuts to first person on the same view. The film is rendered in the game's own engine, recorded, encoded, and played on Play while the game itself downloads and builds behind it. How do films introduce a world, a character and a story in their first minute? What makes sixty seconds of rendered footage read as cinema rather than as a game camera? And how long does the first minute have to be, given what a browser must fetch and build before a player can walk?

Short answer: an opening does five jobs in order, place, person, problem, wrong note, threshold, and a drive does the first and last for free. The person is best introduced with no face: hands, objects and routine, since the player is about to become them. The call works as procedure, not explanation, and the static should take the words that change the meaning of the message. One small unremarked detail before the long hold carries the dread. The cut to control belongs at a physical threshold, with no prompt. On craft, the game's own lens is the biggest problem: its camera sees 112 degrees across, an 8 mm lens, where the films this scene resembles were shot at 27 to 50 mm; the film camera needs a field of view of its own, deep focus, a marine layer in place of noon sun, 24 frames a second with a half-frame shutter, a radio that sounds like a radio, and no score. On length, a first visit to the live game fetches about 52 MB before a player can walk, and a 720p sixty-second video adds 22 to 34 MB; at 10 Mbps that is about 60 to 70 s, at 25 Mbps under 30 s. So sixty seconds is the floor, set by the cut's rhythm rather than by loading, and a held last frame with a quiet progress line covers the slower lines.

01

What an opening has to do

David Bordwell separates two kinds of first shot: the routine establishing shot that only sets a location, and the weighted first shot meant to be read for theme or mood, which he suggests became common once sound arrived, as a way to say "this is cinema" [1]. Blake Snyder's "opening image" is the screenwriter's version of the same idea: a snapshot of tone, scope and the protagonist's state before the story, which the final image answers [2]. Walter Murch's rule of six ranks what a cut must serve: emotion first at 51 percent, then story, rhythm, eye trace, the two-dimensional plane and three-dimensional continuity, sacrificed from the bottom up [3]. Hitchcock's framing rule sits under all of it: the size of an object in the frame should match its importance in the story at that moment [4].

For a sixty-second opening the practical order is: the place, the person, the problem, the wrong note, the threshold. Each shot adds one of those and holds its mood. Slowness reads as confidence when every shot moves from the general to the specific, and as dead air when two shots in a row say the same thing.

02

The drive as the threshold

The drive is the oldest establishing device because it does three jobs at once: it crosses from the ordinary world into the story's, it shows the world's scale against one small vehicle, and it gives the camera time to take an attitude toward the traveller. The Shining opens on eight aerial shots over about two and a half minutes: a fast zoom over a mirrored lake, then the small yellow car on the Going-to-the-Sun Road, tracked, passed, and followed up to the hotel under the Dies Irae; one close reading argues the camera behaves like a bird of prey with "a peculiar interest in the vehicle" [5]. Fargo resolves a car out of a white-out, small and without orientation, "just a slow, almost hypnotic image" [6]. Twin Peaks' titles dissolve through a wren, a sawmill, a welcome sign and slowed falls, the Pacific Northwest register of mist, timber and water, giving "a sense of comfort while simultaneously hinting at the underlying truth" [7]. The Witch inverts it as a departure: the cart leaves the colony's gates, Thomasin looks back, and Eggers holds a static frame as it jolts toward the treeline [8].

The alternative the genre offers is the cold open on the threat itself (Scream, It Follows, It Comes at Night). Day Hike's threat is never shown early, so the drive is the right shape, and it should end at a hard stop: the shoulder, the board.

03

A character with no face

Show the person through what they handle, how they do routine things, and what they do not say. Rear Window gives Jeffries' whole backstory in one pan before a word is spoken: a cast signed with his name, a smashed camera, a photograph of a racing crash. Hitchcock called it "simply using cinematic means to tell a story", better than a line asking how the leg was broken [9]. David Mamet's memo to the writers of The Unit puts the rule bluntly: pretend the characters cannot speak and write a silent film, and any line that amounts to "as you know" is a failure [10].

Games that hand the player a body do the same and go further: they withhold the face. Resident Evil 7 keeps Ethan's face out of every shot as a vessel for the player [11]. Outlast starts in a parked car with an email on the passenger seat, batteries and a camcorder to check before the door opens, so the objects give job, motive and the game's key tool at once [12]. Firewatch introduces Henry through a text prologue because players needed all of that knowledge "in the first five to 10 minutes" [13]. For a character the player is about to inhabit, a face makes a separate person, and hands make a body to step into.

04

The call: procedure, understatement, static

A call is drama when both parties already know the facts and are doing something with them: a check-in, a handover, a request. The audience assembles the story from shorthand, from what is skipped and from tone. Twin Peaks' pilot puts an absurdly fussy switchboard transfer (the black phone, not the brown one, on the table by the red chair) before Pete's "She's dead. Wrapped in plastic", a line Lynch fed to the actor with the camera rolling [14]. Gustav Möller built The Guilty on the discovery that listening to a real emergency call made him see images, a different set for every listener [15]. Radio procedure is plain language now: "go ahead", "stand by", "copy" [16].

The signal that breaks up is a well-worn device, and it works best when the lost words change the meaning. Event Horizon's distress call is first heard as "liberate me", save me, and in full as "libera te tutemet ex inferis", save yourself [17]. That is exactly Day Hike's beat: dispatch says "get back to the road" and the static takes the part that would have said why.

The sound of the call matters as much as the words. A two-way radio passes about 300 Hz to 3 kHz, with saturation, a noise floor, key clicks and a squelch tail of 5 to 100 ms [18], [19]. Murch's "worldizing" for American Graffiti played the radio show through a speaker in real spaces and recorded it again, so the radio sat in the room rather than on top of it [20]. The ranger's own voice stays dry and close; dispatch is band-passed and played through the cab.

05

The wrong note

Plant one small detail, give it no emphasis, and let the audience carry it. Hitchcock's bomb under the table: surprise buys fifteen seconds, suspense fifteen minutes, because the audience "is participating in the secret" [21]. The note has to be unremarked; the moment a character reacts, it becomes plot. Chernobyl's first episode has schoolchildren walk past on a sunny morning while a bird drops dead behind them [22]. Hereditary's first shot pushes into a dollhouse room until it is the real room, and nothing comments on the change of scale [23]. The Witch loses a baby in the three seconds of a game of peekaboo [8]. The daylight register is the one Day Hike shares: understatement at noon.

The static is the big note, so everything else stays quiet. One or two more, none remarked on: no birdsong when the engine stops, a notice on the board newer than the rest.

06

The handoff to first person

There are two schools. Valve never takes control away: Half-Life's tram and Half-Life 2's train deliver the world through a window while the player can look around, and Half-Life 2's station was, in Viktor Antonov's words, "our crucial establishment shot and a big part of our investment" [24]. The horror precedents take control away and give it back at a physical threshold: Outlast at the car door [12], Resident Evil 7 on foot at the end of the drive [11], and Silent Hill 2 from the car at the overlook down a foggy path where unseen noises come from the woods and "before the end of the path you are already scared by sheer atmosphere" [25]. Firewatch is the closest structural precedent: text and short in-engine pieces, a truck, a forested trailhead, a walk in [26].

The school matters less than where the handoff falls. At a door, a gate or a trailhead the cut means "now you go in". Day Hike cuts to first person on the video's last framing, the board in view and the truck behind, then holds with no prompt. The first thing the player does is turn and look, and the forest's sound, or its absence, does the teaching.

07

The camera

Lens. Long lenses compress depth and stack a landscape; wide ones expand space and make movement feel faster and more present [27]. Jarin Blaschke shot 85 percent of The Witch on a 32 mm, "right there with the characters... without getting into distracting Z-axis distortion" [28]. Roger Deakins favours 32 mm on Super 35 with 27 mm as his wide [29]. For cars Shane Hurlbut recommends 27 mm or longer for a polished look [30].

The game's camera has a vertical field of view of 1.4 radians, about 80 degrees, which at 16:9 is 112 degrees across: roughly an 8 mm lens on Super 35. That is right for walking and wrong for every shot of a film. The film camera needs its own field of view, in the engine's own units:

24 mm 55° 32.5° 0.57

the game's camera 112° 80° 1.40

Height and movement. A high angle diminishes the subject and gives "an unsettling predator's point of view" [31]. The Shining's camera comes up behind the car "as if it is another car" and passes it [5]. It Follows played scenes wide, in deep focus, "with minimal coverage", so the audience could watch the background [32]. Deakins on Prisoners: "we didn't need the camera to punctuate the suspense" [33]. A slow push-in builds tension more gradually than a cut to a close-up.

Screen direction. Keep every exterior on one side of the road so the truck always travels the same way across the frame; crossing the line reverses its direction [34]. Kubrick breaks it once in The Shining on purpose.

Focus. A blur gradient on a landscape makes it look nearer and smaller, the miniature effect [35]. Keep wide shots in deep focus; the engine's depth-of-field defaults (f/1.4, 50 mm) are far too shallow for anything but an insert.

Frame. A 2.39:1 image uses 74 percent of a 16:9 screen's height, and 2:1 nearly fills a landscape phone [36]. Encode without black bars and letterbox in the page, with the subtitles in the bar.

08

Rhythm

Average shot length in Hollywood fell from about 12 s to under 4 s over the twentieth century [37]; Bordwell puts the late 1990s at 3 to 6 s [38]. A crowd-sourced figure for horror is 15.7 s, which its own author flags as unverified, so read it as "horror is slower" rather than as a norm [39]. Shots are longest in a film's setups and shortest at its climaxes [40], and a sixty-second opening is all setup. The Shining's drive is eight shots in about 150 s.

For quiet dread in sixty seconds, six to ten shots of six to ten seconds is the defensible range, one or two held longer. Start the radio before its picture (a J-cut) and let static tails carry across cuts (an L-cut) [41]. Hold the last shot a beat longer than comfortable, with no movement for the final two or three seconds, and cut to black on silence.

The eight shots this gives Day Hike:

Shot Seconds Content Sound
1 8 to 10 Wide over the sea stacks and mist, the truck tiny on the coast road; a long lens from high up surf, then the engine, then a squelch
2 6 Along the road from the sea side, the forest to the tarmac, the camera a beat behind the truck dispatch keys up before the cut
3 10 The cab from the back seat: gloved hands, the handset, the empty passenger seat, the road ahead the call
4 5 The handset, the hand that holds it the call
5 6 The truck passes low into the treeline the signal breaks, the static carries over
6 7 A locked-off wide as the truck slows onto the shoulder by the board "say again?", nothing; the engine cuts
7 7 The door, the step onto gravel, the ranger from behind facing the trail boots, wind, no birds
8 8 to 10 A slow push past the ranger's shoulder onto the trail, then held, then black wind, then silence

09

Light, colour and motion

Midday sun is hard top light with deep shadows; crews fly silks as a personal cloud or wait for overcast [42]. The Witch, in Blaschke's words, "works only if it's gloomy" [28], and Prisoners' exteriors are almost all overcast at Villeneuve's insistence [33]. The in-world silk is a marine layer: diffuse sun, soft shadows, far planes fading to pale grey-blue. The game already starts every match in mist, which is the right weather for the film too.

For the grade, muted greens and slightly lifted blacks: Janusz Kaminski flashed Saving Private Ryan at about 15 percent so as not to get "totally sharp blacks" [43]. Teal-and-orange turns greens yellow, so vegetation looks as if it is dying [44]. Aerial perspective, the loss of contrast and saturation with distance and the shift toward blue, is what the mist gives for free [45]. Grain and halation belong to film emulation and can be added lightly at playback; chromatic aberration is disliked in games and adds nothing here.

On motion: 24 frames a second with a 180-degree shutter, an exposure of 1/48 s per frame, is the look of film [46], and a renderer that steps frame by frame can make true motion blur by accumulating several sub-frames over half of each frame, the way Unreal's Movie Render Queue does [47]. The cost is judder on 60 Hz screens, which show 24 fps frames for alternately 50 and 33 ms, so camera moves must stay slow: at most one frame width every seven seconds [48]. A long lens on the truck keeps the background's pan slow.

10

Sound

Sound carries most of the dread in this scene. The bed for a car is layered, engine, tyre roll, interior road tone, wind, and sits 6 to 10 dB under the dialogue, with the perspective flipped at every cut between inside and outside [49]. The exterior bed is surf, wind in conifers and the truck passing. The loudest moment in the film is the engine cutting out.

No score, or almost none. Carter Burwell's music for No Country for Old Men totals about sixteen minutes; the violin cues he tried "destroyed the tension that came from the quiet", and what survived was "snuck in underneath the wind or the sound of a car" [50]. If Day Hike has any music, it is one low note that enters at the break-up and ends with the engine. Silent Hill taught a generation that radio static means danger [51], which is a reason to let the static stand alone.

Levels: dialogue-peak rules vary widely between practitioners, and the loudness standards differ by platform, from Netflix's −27 LKFS to YouTube's −14 LUFS normalisation [52]. The video should be mastered to match the game's own loudness, measured rather than assumed, with the dialogue 6 to 10 dB over the bed.

11

Performance, titles and subtitles

The ranger cannot act with a face today, and does not need to. Posture, head turns and hands read at any distance; eyes and lip sync need a face rig, and a realistic face done badly is the Polar Express problem. The Kuleshov effect, a cut from a neutral face to what it looks at, has been reproduced under controlled conditions [53], so a cut from the ranger's back to the handset, the empty seat or the board does the acting. Dispatch is never seen, so no lip sync is needed at all. The animation budget goes on the few moves with weight: the reach for the handset, the door, the step onto gravel, the pause.

The title comes after the film: a cold open, a cut to black, then the game's name for three or four seconds over wind [54]. Subtitles are on by default. Netflix's English style guide allows 42 characters a line, two lines, and up to 20 characters a second, with a voice heard through a device in italics [55]; Xbox's guidelines add a speaker label such as "(on radio)", mixed case, a sans-serif face and a minimum of 26 px at 1080p [56], where accessibility practitioners ask for 46 px [57]. At 20 characters a second, a 25-second call is about 500 characters, roughly 90 words: the script's budget.

12

The file, and how long the first minute has to be

Encoding. Apple's streaming guidance puts H.264 at 6.0 to 7.8 Mbps for 1080p and 3.0 to 4.5 Mbps for 720p, with a key frame every two seconds [58]; for sixty seconds that is 45 to 59 MB at 1080p and 22 to 34 MB at 720p. In x264 a constant-rate factor of 18 to 20 is close to visually lossless, each step of 6 halves the bitrate, and +faststart lets the file play as it arrives [59]. Fog bands in 8-bit video: the fix is triangular dither at render time [60], aq-mode 3 and -tune grain at encode, and Netflix's CAMBI score as the gate, where about 5 is "slightly annoying" [61]. An AV1 version in 10-bit avoids the banding outright and carries grain as synthesis, but Safari decodes it only on recent Apple hardware [62], so H.264 stays the fallback. Encode at 2.39:1 or 2:1, no bars, and letterbox in the page.

What the game loads. Measured on the live game on 2026-09-29, a first visit fetches about 52 MB before a player can walk: 47 MB of models, of which the four trees are 2.5 to 3 MB each, 3.6 MB of ground maps, 0.7 MB of JavaScript and a little sound; on WebGPU another 3 MB of shader translators and map. The title page fetched 49 MB of that before Play, because it built a full renderer for its backdrop; making the title page a still or a looping clip moves nearly all of it behind the intro, which is where it belongs. On this machine, with everything cached, the build after Play blocks for about two seconds and settles in five; a mid-range laptop is two to three times that.

How long, then. Ookla's global medians are about 104 Mbps for fixed broadband and past 90 Mbps for mobile, and the United States' fixed median is over 300 Mbps [63]; medians of speed-test users flatter the typical player, and a laptop on wi-fi at the far end of a house or a phone in a poor cell is far slower. Designing for a floor of 10 Mbps:

Connection The game, 52 MB With a 720p video, 28 MB With a 1080p video, 52 MB
100 Mbps 4 s 6 s 8 s
25 Mbps 17 s 26 s 33 s
10 Mbps 42 s 64 s 83 s
5 Mbps 83 s 128 s 166 s

So a 720p film at 2:1, near 20 MB, lands with the game inside sixty seconds at the 10 Mbps floor, and the film's length is set by its cut, six to ten shots, sixty seconds at the least, not by the load. Two things cover the lines below the floor: the video streams and starts within a couple of seconds, the game's download beginning once the video has a few seconds in hand so the two do not fight; and the last frame holds, with one quiet line and a thin bar saying what is still being fetched or built, until the world is ready. The player then clicks to step out, which is the gesture the browser needs anyway for the mouse and the sound.

Mesh compression is the lever outside the film: the models are served gzipped, which takes only 23 percent off, and the glTF mesh compressions usually halve them, which would move every row of that table.

13

What Day Hike will do

  • Eight shots, sixty seconds, the order in section 8; no face; hands, the handset, the empty seat; the truck always crossing the frame the same way.
  • A film camera of its own: 0.43 radians vertical as the default (32 mm), 0.10 to 0.14 for the coastal wide, nothing wider than 0.57; deep focus everywhere but the handset insert.
  • Recorded at 24 frames a second with a half-frame shutter by sub-frame accumulation, in mist, graded to muted greens with lifted blacks, grain and halation light and added at playback.
  • The call as procedure in plain language, about 90 words; dispatch band-passed, keyed, with squelch tails, played through the cab; the ranger dry; the static taking the words that explain "get back to the road"; "say again?" met by nothing; the engine cut as the loudest beat; no score.
  • The title after the cut to black. Subtitles on, from the scene's own timeline rather than burnt in, dispatch in italics with a radio label, in the letterbox bar.
  • A 720p H.264 file at 2:1, faststart, key frames every two seconds, dithered, gated on banding, with an AV1 version where it plays; the title page made light; the game fetched behind the film; a thin bar and one line for what is still loading; a click to step out.
