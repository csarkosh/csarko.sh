How Horror Directors Build Dread Shot by Shot

How Hitchcock, Kubrick, Carpenter, Nakata, Aster and Eggers build dread: the three levers, a feeling-to-technique table, 30 director profiles, 168 sources.

  • 40 min read

Part 4 of 7 in Horror craft · Next: 23 Horror Sequences Broken Down Shot by Shot

Question: How do the most renowned horror directors, classic, Japanese and Korean, and modern, together with their cinematographers and editors, design shot sequences and cinematic composition (shot size, angle, lens, depth of field, aspect ratio, framing, negative space, depth staging, lighting and darkness, camera movement and stillness, point of view, editing rhythm and cut types, and sound with image) to create specific feelings and perspectives in the audience and to guide the story, and which of their methods are general film craft a filmmaker can apply to any film?

Short answer: Horror directors build feeling by controlling three things at once: what the audience knows relative to the characters, where the audience's eye is at each moment, and how long the audience is made to wait. Dread and suspense come from giving the viewer more knowledge than the character, then holding a wide, deep, often static frame until the viewer finds the threat before anyone on screen does: Hitchcock's bomb under the table, the background figure in Halloween and It Follows, the empty door-space in Longlegs. Shock is the opposite: a long quiet lead-in, then a sub-second cut to a close-up on a loud sting, used so rarely it is never anticipated. Confinement comes from wide lenses placed close and tall or square frames; the uncanny from a familiar space made wrong and a figure that moves the wrong way; presence from a smooth, operator-less camera. Editors converge on one rhythm: a slow, flat cutting baseline for dread, short bursts of fast cutting for violence, and music removed wherever the picture already carries the fear. Measured evidence supports a few of these claims and leaves most lens, angle and framing doctrine as craft lore; several famous production facts remain disputed. The 23 sequences behind these conclusions are broken down on a companion page, 23 Horror Sequences Broken Down Shot by Shot.

01

How shots and sequences create feeling and guide a story

A shot frightens or reassures an audience through three levers that every director profiled below manipulates knowingly: the distribution of knowledge between viewer and character, the control of the viewer's gaze, and the control of time. Hitchcock stated the first lever in its canonical form. A bomb that simply explodes under a table gives the public "fifteen seconds of surprise"; a bomb the public knows about, with a clock in view, turns banal conversation into "fifteen minutes of suspense," so "whenever possible the public must be informed," the exception being a twist whose surprise is itself the point [1], [2]. David Bordwell shows the idea predates Hitchcock (Lessing) and that Hitchcock did not always obey it: Rebecca, Suspicion and Psycho lean on surprise [2]. Bordwell's vocabulary is the useful one for a working filmmaker: restricted narration confines the audience to what one character knows, which lets you delay the creature's reveal and build empathy and loss, while unrestricted narration lets the audience "feel apprehension and suspense about what the characters don't know is happening" [3]. Murray Smith adds the machinery: alignment is produced by spatio-temporal attachment (whom the camera follows) and subjective access (whose feelings we are given), and it is separate from allegiance, our moral evaluation; a viewer "may align with a character but not necessarily ally with them" [4].

The second lever is the gaze, and here the craft meets measurement. Viewers of edited film look at the same places at the same time: gaze defaults to screen centre, faces and motion, and synchrony rises about ten frames, roughly 333 ms, after each cut; in Loschky and colleagues' Moonraker experiment the film's exogenous pull on attention was 1.5 to 6 times stronger than the effect of what viewers understood about the story [5]. Tim Smith's eye-tracking of a long static take in There Will Be Blood found that blocking and glances steer gaze "as precisely as a rapidly edited sequence of close-up shots," giving viewers "an illusion of volition" [6]. This is the empirical basis for the horror long take: the audience believes it is scanning freely while staging decides where it looks, so a background entrance can be placed exactly where the gaze is not; every framing device in Section 2 either exploits that default or works against it. Hitchcock's Rear Window demonstration is the Kuleshov effect in miniature: a close-up of James Stewart smiling at a dog reads as kindly; the identical close-up after a shot of a half-dressed woman reads as "a dirty old man" [7]. Three controlled replications since 2006 confirm that context shots steer how a neutral face is read, and one shows music alone does it [8], [9].

The third lever is time. Val Lewton's formula for his RKO unit was "A love story, three scenes of suggested horror and one of actual violence. Fadeout," because "You can't keep up horror that's long sustained" and "there should be no more than four or five in a picture" [10], [11]. The modern editors say the same thing. Louise Ford, who cuts Robert Eggers' films: tension "is holding the shot as long as you possibly can," and "Every time you make a cut, that is a break" [12], [13]. Greg Ng on Longlegs: "the shots are held so long that there's an expectation of something to happen," and "You don't need to jam it down people's throats if they're already feeling tense" [14]. Kirk Baxter, cutting Fincher, supplies the counterweight: "If you sit on it too long then the tension may fade away" [15], [16]. The hold works only while the audience has been given a reason to wait, which is Hitchcock's visible bomb. Nick Redfern's cut-density analyses of Halloween and Friday the 13th show the resulting rhythm in data: a flat, slow cutting baseline "organised around creating a pervasive sense of dread punctuated by transitory moments of violence," with sharp local spikes at each attack [17].

Sound is the lever that reaches the space the frame does not show. Michel Chion's acousmatic sound is "sound one hears without seeing their originating cause," and the acousmêtre is the one "not-yet-seen, but who remains liable to appear in the visual field at any moment"; showing the source usually drains its power, which is why horror delays it [18], [19]. Noël Burch's six segments of off-screen space (the four frame edges, behind the camera, behind the set) give the formal map; Dean Cundey calls the same thing "lurking space" at the edges of an anamorphic frame [20], [21]. Lewton's statement of the principle is the oldest: "if you make the screen dark enough, the mind's eye will read anything into it you want" [11].

Finally, these devices guide the story because each one marks a transfer: Psycho's pull-back from Marion's dead eye to the Bates house hands the point of view to Norman, and the crane that rises away from the child in a clown suit at the end of Halloween's opening rereads every preceding beat. Larkin Seiple, the cinematographer of Weapons, describes the one camera move in that film that "breaks the mold" by showing "more than is necessary for the character," a plain pan while a woman sleeps in her car, as the moment the audience is told it knows more than she does [22]. Julia Ducournau says of Raw that "In significant spots in the movie, you'll see a track forward and that means something" [23]. The reserved move, the one shot of its kind in a sequence, is a recurring release valve across the sequences on the companion page: Hitchcock's single overhead on the Arbogast stairs, Spielberg's one dolly zoom on the beach, Carpenter's one crane, Wan's one scene in which "we stopped the camera from moving" [24]. The theory maps onto this grid. Julian Hanich's five types of pleasurable fear sit along the knowledge axis: suggested horror (hinted, imagined), cinematic dread (anticipation of something not yet shown), cinematic shock (startle plus the expectation of the unpleasant), cinematic terror (monster visible, survival uncertain) and direct horror (shown graphically) [25], [26]. Freud's uncanny requires "a conflict of judgement whether things which have been 'surmounted' and are regarded as incredible are not, after all, possible," which is why a world that first coincides with ours and then departs from it in one particular disturbs more than a fairy-tale world [27].

Three points about the table. First, the "dread school" and the "jump-scare school" of the last decade share almost all of their hardware (large-format sensors with wide lenses used close, long takes, practical creatures) and differ in how tension is released: James Wan describes a two-stage machine, "I tried to creep you out at first, create this palpable sense of dread," then "you're jumping out of your seat" [84], while David Robert Mitchell "moved away from jump scares and wanted suspense built from visible danger in the background" [32] and Ari Aster told his editor he was "completely against the 'jump-scare' device" [85]. Second, the lens rows depend on each other: Charlie Sarroff explains that large format lets Smile "shoot on extremely wide lenses with minimal distortion," because on smaller formats "people's faces become unrealistic, even ghoulish, when in close on wide lenses," and the extra height of a 2:1 ratio gives the empty headroom the dread row needs [45]. Third, the best-attested audience behaviour for any framing choice is Polanski's doorway in Rosemary's Baby: William Fraker framed Ruth Gordon fully visible on the bed, Polanski moved the camera so the jamb hid her, and Fraker understood only when he "saw everyone leaning in their seats, trying to look around the edge of the door frame" [46]. Obstructed negative space produces a physical search, which is Bazin's and Bordwell's account of depth staging as induced participation [86].

04

Classic directors: Hitchcock, Lewton and Tourneur, Murnau, Clayton, Bava, Polanski, Kubrick, Friedkin, Carpenter, Argento, Hooper, Scott

Section 7 tabulates each director's signatures; the profiles here and in the next two sections add the stated intentions and the production facts behind them.

Alfred Hitchcock (John L. Russell; editor George Tomasini). The audience should know more than the characters, meaning is made by cutting a face against what it sees, and violence is fragmented montage planned in advance. Psycho was shot almost entirely on 50 mm lenses for a field of view close to human vision [44]. The storyboard legend needs qualifying: Bill Krohn found the "storyboarded to the finest detail" reputation was "to a great degree perpetuated by Hitchcock himself or the publicity arm of the studios," and Hitchcock told Ebert that "in shooting it you lose perhaps 40 per cent of your original conception" [87].

Val Lewton, Jacques Tourneur, Mark Robson and Robert Wise. Tourneur meant to suggest the cat only through shadow, shooting so that "you couldn't really be sure what you were seeing," and the front office forced real-cat shots on him; on Night of the Demon the producer inserted demon shots over his objection [61], [88]. Wise carried the rule into The Haunting (1963): the supernatural kept off screen "following Val Lewton's view that people fear the unknown more than what they can see," on a deliberately distorting 30 mm wide-angle in brightly lit rooms with ceilings [89]. That bright, wide, mobile version of the rule is the one Nakata studied before Ring and the one Wan calls "the movie that has the biggest influence on me" [90], [84].

F. W. Murnau (Fritz Arno Wagner). Nosferatu's shadow substitutes for the body: Orlok climbs the stairs as a hunched shadow, the shadow reaches for the doorknob while his hand never enters frame, and the shadow's hand grips Ellen's heart; the make-up intensifies gradually so the audience never habituates [91].

Jack Clayton and Freddie Francis (The Innocents, 1961). Style as work-around: Fox forced CinemaScope, early Scope lenses could not focus closer than ten feet, so interiors were shot at f/16 and f/22 with candles built up to five wicks and "a man on a dimmer hidden in a grandfather clock," and the width was then undone with hand-painted graduated filters "being slid in from either side," which "greatly enhanced the claustrophobic feeling" [48].

Mario Bava and Georges Franju. Bava used unmotivated multi-gel colour ("frankly artificial, and Bava makes no attempt to hide it") a decade before Suspiria [92], and in Kill, Baby, Kill cast a boy as the ghost child, with "some Melissa shots" filmed "performing actions in reverse, which gives her movements an uncanny quality," the earliest sourced reversed ghost [71]. Franju called Eyes Without a Face "horror in homeopathic doses" and found the most beautiful horror film he had seen to be a documentary of a trepanation in which the patient smiled [80], [78].

Roman Polanski (William Fraker, Gilbert Taylor). Fraker's rule is "depth, not just a flat field": Rosemary's Baby was shot on 18 mm or 25 mm, Polanski rejecting a diffused 40 mm because he wanted "the apartment to feel like it was closing in on Rosemary"; elements were hidden so a lateral move with close foregrounds "would reveal something new"; and the final shot has no baby, carried by Mia Farrow's expression ("there will be no baby") [46], [93].

Stanley Kubrick (John Alcott, Garrett Brown; editor Ray Lovejoy). Brown documents sets built without flyaway walls so the Steadicam could "travel unobtrusively from space to space or lurk in the shadows ... with a menacing presence," and the rule that the camera "feels better if the camera can be disembodied and not required to climb stairs itself" [31]. Alcott lit by practicals because the 360-degree camera "ruled out floor and ceiling lights" [68]. The bright, unshadowed, symmetrical hotel is thus a consequence of the camera method as much as a decision, on a set built, per Jan Harlan, "very deliberately" to be "offbeat and off the track" [94].

William Friedkin (Owen Roizman). The Exorcist's realism is a lighting rule: "a realistic, available-light look," every light with "a visible, realistic source," the bedroom drained to a monochrome so skin was the only colour [77], [95]. The one anti-realist device, the two-to-three-frame demon face, was added in the cutting room and defended as perceptual realism, how intrusive images "pop quickly into our mind's eye" [96], [95]. Friedkin reached for long lenses at violence ("A tighter lens had more impact than a wide shot") against a neutral documentary field, so the change of focal length is itself the signal [95].

John Carpenter (Dean Cundey). Cundey establishes three devices: the Panaglide reserved for Michael, anamorphic used "to create a whole world" with foregrounds that make even the widest vistas "claustrophobic," and light withheld, in The Thing the eye light normally present as "a little white sparkle" dropped once the creature's identity is set so the eyes stay dark and the audience "feels it" before anything happens, while the creature's silhouette was lit on the back wall with small lights keeping it "a little mysterious" [38], [97], [98].

Dario Argento (Luciano Tovoli). Suspiria's negative was Eastman 5254 printed by Technicolor's dye-transfer process, so the popular "last film shot in three-strip Technicolor" is wrong, the defensible claim being the last Italian film printed by imbibition [99], [100], [101]. Argento aimed at "the colour of Walt Disney's Snow White" [101]. Tenebrae inverts the palette on principle: "Caring about shadows seemed ridiculous to me and, more than that, reassuring" [69]. Fear is relocated from darkness to exposure, and the autonomous crane and the gloved-hand POV are two ends of one device, a camera that behaves like an intelligence with intent [102].

Tobe Hooper (Daniel Pearl). Hooper's aim was documentary ("the most important thing was the sound of truth"), with Vietnam footage and local news as the influences [103]. Eliot Rockett, who later shot for Hooper, credits him with the lesson that "seat jumper" moments come from keeping the camera static and not cutting away [104].

Ridley Scott (Derek Vanlint; editor Terry Rawlings). Scott storyboarded the whole film and wanted it "directional," "low-key," "gloomy," against 2001's high key [105]. His principle: "The most important thing in a film of this type is not what you see, but the effect of what you think you saw," the creature mostly in shadow and altering shape "so you never really know exactly what he looks like," because "Whenever you get to the monster in a horror film it's a big let-down" [75], [106].

George Romero, David Cronenberg, Wes Craven. Romero's "frenetic style" came from covering himself with cuts ("if you've got a master you're stuck with what you've got"); Night borrowed the grainy black-and-white authority of a newsreel and the "real hard shadows and weird angles" of horror comics [107], [108]. Mark Irwin, Cronenberg's cinematographer, states the most transferable lighting rule in the set: "not to give them away as horror films in terms of lighting," Veronica warm while Brundle's light grows harsher [51]; Cronenberg wants films seen "from the point of view of the disease" [109]. Craven's contributions are structural (dream and waking blurred, the family as the seat of terror, the self-aware slasher) and practical, camera tricks built into the set so the dream could be photographed straight [110].

05

Japanese and Korean directors: Nakata, Kurosawa, Shimizu, Miike, Na Hong-jin, Kim Jee-woon, Bong

Hideo Nakata (Junichiro Hayashi). Nakata strips the cursed video of spatial reference points so "you are not able to tell what is what," and shoots "bits and pieces" because "how to edit the scene is already in my mind when I shoot it" [111], [112]. His statement of the J-horror grammar: the ghost "appears faintly as an apparition behind you, often out of focus," seen as a reflection on a switched-off TV and gone when you turn, and "It just feels too fake if there is too much blood" [40].

Kiyoshi Kurosawa (Tokusho Kikumura on Cure). Kurosawa's statements concern sound and the off-screen: "Sound is what defines the place that is neither story nor reality, but in between," and his sounds come from the real world rather than synthesizers to express "the world that lies beyond what is visible on-screen" [113], [74]. Critics document the camera: he films from afar rather than at a startling shoulder level, treats doors, windows and monitors as portals, and lets calm give way to brutality "with almost no marked boundary" [74], [70].

Takashi Shimizu. The most useful articulation of the J-horror intent: what is scariest is "The presence of the ghost," because attacking ghosts are "more toward the monster ... if you can touch them, why don't you just run away from them?"; but "once we have to present in a film, we can't just have a presence. We have to have it attacking" [114]. He prefers practical effects because "as soon as they figure out that it's a CGI, it's not going to be scary any more" [115].

Takashi Miike (Hideo Yamamoto). Audition is a betrayal of expectation whose horror sits in performance rather than gore: "there are terrifying things in life too and they are all made by human beings," and "For me, Audition is not horror. At least, there is no monster" [53].

Na Hong-jin (Hong Kyung-pyo; editor Kim Sun-min). Na controls every frame while abandoning the plan: "I disregarded my storyboard after seeing him search through the woods"; Hong's credo is "Light is more important than the lens. In the end, cinematography is the work of giving shape to light" [116], [117]. Na's threat is hidden rather than dynamic ("I felt that this would be more terrifying than a dynamic threat"), and the stranger was cast to resemble Koreans so his otherness is revealed slowly, "the fear coming from this impossibility of communication" [118].

Kim Jee-woon (Lee Mo-gae). A two-mode colour system: saturated patterns for the gothic ghost story, where the patterns are repressed memories that "will come up and torture you," then muted, hazy-grey, Zodiac-like contrast for I Saw the Devil, with darks grey rather than flat black "to create a creepy atmosphere" [119], [120].

Bong Joon-ho (Kim Hyung-koo). The Host is the documented counter-case to suggestion: a small, clumsy, acrobatic creature shown early, its "small size, clumsiness, and physical otherness" read as "inversions of monster movie conventions" [121]. Across these directors two lineages run: a withholding line (Murnau to Lewton to Wise to Clayton to Nakata, Kurosawa and Shimizu) and a showing line (Bava, Franju, Romero, Cronenberg, Miike, Bong), and even the withholding line ends, as Lewton's formula prescribes, with one scene of actual violence.

Modern directors: Aster, Eggers, Peele, Mitchell, Wan, Flanagan, Kent, Perkins, Cregger and others

Ari Aster (Pawel Pogorzelski; editors Jennifer Lame and Lucian Johnston). Hereditary's stage sets with removable walls produce the dollhouse look, with "wider lenses as the movie goes on" so the family "feel like dolls trapped inside"; lighting hardens in the third act but stays "grounded in the reality" [122], [123], [124]. Johnston notes that on Beau Is Afraid fast cutting was used for interiority and anxiety, a different register from dread [85], [55].

Robert Eggers (Jarin Blaschke; editor Louise Ford). Each film is built around a period ratio and emulsion: The Lighthouse's 1.19 was Blaschke's pitch, "mostly as a joke," which Eggers wrote into the script, with Lang's M as the model for "how visual information was withheld" [47], [125]; Nosferatu returned to 1.66 because Caspar David Friedrich's landscapes are "a bit boxy," and exposed moonlight so heavily on the blue layer that it approaches what is seen when "only the rod cells in our eyes are working" [126]. Ford's editing rules are the dread school's charter: hold as long as possible, cut dry first ("if there wasn't going to be music, how would you make this scary?"), open scenes on an empty frame, cut hard on loud sound, and remember that a hold can overshoot into time not passing [13], [12], [127].

Jordan Peele (Toby Oliver, Mike Gioulakis, Hoyte van Hoytema; editor Nicholas Monsour). On Us, Gioulakis used a low-angle prism to put the lens six inches lower than normal, "a constant reminder about what's underneath, death," and the climactic confrontation was locked off so Peele could "avoid the spectacle" of two Lupitas in one frame [37]. For Nope, van Hoytema set out to "capture the darkness of the night but somehow see through it," pairing an infrared camera with a 65 mm film camera [128].

David Robert Mitchell (Mike Gioulakis). It Follows is the cleanest modern statement of Hitchcock's informing principle done by staging rather than cutting: deep focus, tableaux, long masters without cutaways, a camera meant to feel "distant, sterile" so the audience is less aware of a person behind the lens, and night exteriors lit so the eye can see far; Gregory Crewdson's lit suburban tableaux are the named influence [32].

James Wan (John R. Leonetti, Don Burgess). Wan is the one director in this set who describes the dread-then-jump architecture as a deliberate two-stage machine and defends it ("I like that sadism!") [84]. The Crooked Man was Javier Botet as "a human stop motion puppet"; Wan's favourite choice in the sequel was the one scene where the camera stopped moving, and his stated preference, if forced to pick, is sound over picture [24], [129].

Mike Flanagan (Michael Fimognari; sound designer Trevor Gates). "Two Storms" is the benchmark horror long take (companion page); Gates's silence-as-budget rule and the slowing-sound cue for a ghost's presence are the most precise sound statements in the modern record [50]. Claims about the show's hidden background ghosts rest on fan guides, which disagree about counts and placement [130].

Jennifer Kent (Radek Ladczuk). The Babadook is the most explicit documented template of camera grammar following a psychological arc: five chapters (anxiety, fear, terror, possession, courage), the camera static and Amelia centred on a 32 mm early, handheld arriving with her emotion, a "floating" Steadicam later, chaotic movement at the end, light hardening "motivated by fear," and every creature appearance shot stop-motion five frames at a time [72].

Osgood Perkins (Andrés Arochi; editors Greg Ng and Graham Fortin). Longlegs gives Agent Harker a wider lens than everyone else so "she becomes different from everyone else," and leaves blank space because "fear lives behind your back. It's not in front of you" [28]. The editors describe the standing note "Do we need that?", a process of removing music ("We need more crickets!"), and a one-second Polaroid flash added "because everyone's waiting" [14].

Zach Cregger (Zach Kuperstein, Larkin Seiple; editor Joe Murphy). Barbarian is "Fincher upstairs, Raimi downstairs"; Weapons refuses coverage and establishing shots and gives each chapter its protagonist's camera grammar, with a "malicious" camera that shows "information but it's not gonna help you" and a 12-to-13-frame ceiling scare [82], [64], [131].

Parker Finn (Charlie Sarroff), Ti West (Eliot Rockett), Fede Álvarez and Rodo Sayagues (Pedro Luque). Smile shot on the Alexa 65 so a 28 mm could be used close "with very little warping," at 2:1 for extra height [45]. Rockett lit X "almost exclusively" with period-correct fixtures as an emulation of a late-1970s genre film and turned Pearl to "hardcore Technicolor," with shadows knowingly "goofy" because the referenced style's conventions outrank plausibility [132]. Luque's single-take philosophy is "no way out" rather than virtuosity: the Don't Breathe house tour establishes the space so clearly that the audience knows there is no exit [49]; Álvarez made the film as "less blood and more suspense," an "exercise in reversal" after Evil Dead [133].

Danny and Michael Philippou (Aaron McLisky), Coralie Fargeat (Benjamin Kračun), Guillermo del Toro (Dan Laustsen), Julia Ducournau (Ruben Impens), Demián Rugna. McLisky kept Talk to Me's domestic light motivated and introduced an unmotivated source only at each possession, never "completely surreal" [134]. Fargeat shoots close-ups first and "loves for a shot to develop" [76]. Laustsen never lets shadows go fully black ("then you don't see anything"); del Toro makes the camera moves with the grips himself "because he's the only one who knows where to cut" [135], [136]. Ducournau splits a shot-reverse between a hectic handheld roommate and a "super still" Justine ("she's death and he's life"), looks into the lens four times, and uses the track forward as a marked event [54], [23]. Rugna shows the violence in full and withholds only the when: the first kill is "extremely hard as evidence that I'm threatening the audience all the time," and "horror movies have to have the same rhythm as a comedy" [79], [137]; jump scares, in his view, are a symptom of "a script that really does not scare you" [138].

Found footage: Myrick and Sánchez, Peli, Balagueró and Plaza, Reeves, Savage. The makers converge on three rules. The operator must be a character with a reason to film: "the actors were the cameramen and all the dialogue was improvised" on The Blair Witch Project; Micah buys a camera to review the tapes [139], [41]. The format licenses withholding: "You're not really seeing anything, your imagination is doing all the work" [139]. Credibility outranks coverage: Peli spent about ten months tweaking the fixed bedroom frame because "a few seconds here and there" decide between fright and boredom; Savage's test is "does this need to be a found footage movie?" and his named limit is "you can't have a conventional score" [41], [140]. The Blair Witch cut to black survived a confused test audience because, in Daniel Myrick's words, "What makes us fearful is something that's out of the ordinary, unexplained" [141]. The killer-POV ancestor is Black Christmas, where Bob Clark wondered whether he could make a film "where you never saw the killer" and the operator played the stalker with his own hands in frame [142].

08

What the empirical evidence supports versus craft lore

The measured record is narrower than the doctrine, and a filmmaker should know which claims rest on which. On shot scale, Cutting and Armstrong's analysis of about 31,000 shots in 24 films found that filmmakers already tune duration to scale (extreme long shots average about 8 seconds, extreme close-ups about 2.5) and that larger faces are read faster, with clutter slowing recognition of distant faces by around 200 ms [144]. Rooney and Bálint found that emotional close-ups raise viewers' tendency to attribute mental states to the character shown, but neutral close-ups do nothing and the effect does not transfer to other characters; "mere exposure to close-up faces of characters does not enhance general theory of mind ability" [145]. The usable prior for horror is that a wide shot hiding a threat in clutter needs longer on screen than a reaction close-up, which is the tableau rhythm It Follows and Hereditary adopt; "close-ups create empathy, wides create dread" as a general rule is lore.

On camera angle, the lab record supports "low reads as potent, high as weak" for a character inside a story (Kraft 1987; Sevenants and d'Ydewalle 2006), and Kraft also found that viewers forget the angle while retaining its effect, the strongest evidence that angle works pre-reflectively [146], [147]. For direct address the picture reverses: Baranowski and Hecht found eye level maximises trust and both departures reduce it (low angle M = 4.27, eye level M = 5.03) [148].

On editing, the Kuleshov effect failed one 1992 replication but has been reproduced in three within-subject designs since; Barratt and colleagues' eye-tracked study concluded "some sort of Kuleshov effect does in fact exist," and Baranowski and Hecht showed music alone shifts the reading of a neutral face (partial eta-squared .46), a direct basis for the horror habit of scoring a blank reaction shot [8], [9], [149]. Hollywood's average shot length fell from about 10 seconds in the 1930s and 1940s to under 4 after 2000 across 160 films, the trend Bordwell names "intensified continuity"; horror was not in that sample, and the one horror figure in circulation (15.7 seconds, from crowd-sourced Cinemetrics data that the author himself calls "a bit of an inexact science") should be treated as unreliable [150], [151], [152]. Average shot length is not a measure of cutting rate: Redfern's cut-density plots of Halloween and Friday the 13th show the genre's real signature, a flat slow baseline with local spikes at violence, and the 2009 Friday remake's flatter rate loses the original's structure [17], [153]. Murch's Rule of Six (emotion 51 percent, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, two-dimensional plane 5, three-dimensional space 4) is a triage order, not a measurement (Murch jokes he "had to make the numbers jump around"); its practical content is that spatial continuity is the first thing to sacrifice, the lever horror pulls when it wants discomfort [154], [155].

On gaze, the evidence is strong: attentional synchrony after cuts, centre and face bias, the "tyranny of film" over comprehension, and the finding that centre bias is mostly the photographer's framing rather than an eye-motor artefact [5], [156], [157]. A 2025 preprint that eye-tracked viewers through The Conjuring 2 found jump scares shorten fixations, suppress blinks and enlarge pupils, but gaze did not become more synchronous during scares and half the participants reported looking away; it is not yet peer reviewed and does not report where viewers looked [158].

On camera movement, Heimann, Gallese and colleagues found the Steadicam produces the strongest motor-cortex (mu-rhythm) response of any camera condition, surviving the removal of any actor from the scene, which they offer as "first empirical ground" for the camera simulating the viewer's presence [159], [160], [161]. The one study that measured emotion directly, Yilmaz and colleagues, found movement raised involvement and the sense that "the camera was one's own eyes" but had no significant effect on arousal or valence, including in its horror scene [162]. So the glide is demonstrably more involving, not demonstrably scarier; "stillness after movement lands" is documented as practice (Brown's settled end frames, Peli's timing) and untested as a hypothesis [31], [41].

On the jump scare, a 2026 review found that of 18 startle studies only three used film at all, and in each the startle still came from a laboratory probe tone rather than the film; its useful formalisation is that a jump scare is multimodal, has both a time point and a time span (the build-up shapes the response), and is multivalent, negative and enjoyable at once, with earlier work showing anticipation potentiates startle [163]. Joe Dzuban's practitioner anatomy (a lull of around 15 seconds, pre-scare sounds "rounded," the hit "sharp, strident ... loud and proud," and "Much of the success of a scare comes from picture editing") is consistent with that evidence but is itself craft [59].

Everything else in the lens, ratio and framing vocabulary is lore plus optics. Perspective distortion is a function of camera-to-subject distance, not of the lens, which is why "wide and close" bulges walls and enlarges approach speed, but no controlled study of focal length, depth of field, aspect ratio, negative space, short-siding, headroom or symmetry on viewers' emotion was found [164], [86], [165]. Jay Holben's lighting rules (sell "a 'no-light' look" with edge light and a soft base two or three stops under key; "Don't be afraid to have overexposed elements in dark scenes" because hot accents make blacks read crisper) are the trade's consensus, stated as practice, not findings [29].

Source caveats, disputed facts and remaining gaps

Several of the production facts behind these profiles are contested in print. Suspiria was not "shot in three-strip Technicolor"; it was shot on Eastman 5254 and printed by dye transfer [100], [101]. The number and placement of The Exorcist's subliminal demon frames differ between sources, and no authoritative frame log exists [166]. The Cat People bus is credited variously to Robson, Tourneur and Lewton [167], [61]. Hitchcock's storyboard legend is partly studio publicity [87]. Blair Witch's shooting span is given as eight intensive days and as two weeks [139]. Hill House's hidden ghosts have no count from the filmmakers, and fan guides disagree [130]. The disputes that belong to particular set pieces (the Psycho shower counts and authorship, the jungle-gym crows, the Alien chestburster's "one take," the It Follows hallway pan, Sadako's reversed walk) are set out on the companion page.

Some claims rest on excerpts rather than full texts. Nakata's statement that he watched The Haunting at least five times is relayed through an encyclopaedia summary of a newspaper interview [90]. Several cinematographer interviews were readable only in reprints or summaries, and the Longlegs editor's and cinematographer's remarks come from a reprint and a podcast summary [28], [168]. Wan's defence that "a great jump scare is really hard to design" is relayed second hand [129]. Hanich's five fear types are reported through a publisher's chapter list and a Danish-language summary [25], [26]; Burch's six zones through a thesis quoting the French edition [20]; Chion's four powers of the acousmêtre through summaries without page numbers [19].

The remaining gaps are specific. No verbatim statement by Carpenter or Cundey on placing Michael at the edge of frame or in the far background was found; the depth-staging reading is critics'. No primary statement by Kurosawa on camera distance or static takes, or by Shimizu on Kayako's choreography, was reached. Flanagan's reasoning about what the Hill House long take does to a viewer, as opposed to how it was made, is not in any reachable text. On the empirical side there is no peer-reviewed horror-specific average-shot-length study by era, no controlled comparison of dread under slow versus fast cutting of the same material, no eye-tracking study that manipulates a horror framing variable (negative space, depth placement of a threat), no study of stillness after movement or of killer-POV versus objective framing, and no physiological study of the jump scare as cut, with sting timing relative to the picture cut; the 2026 review explicitly proposes one [163]. Claims about infrasound and low-frequency rumble are common in blogs and were not sourced to any primary study, so they are omitted.
