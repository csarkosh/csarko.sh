A painted look over a photoreal render: WebGPU against WebGL2

A painterly post chain on WebGPU and WebGL2: the same look on both, but a stroke filter written with sector arrays cost 12.5 ms on WebGPU against 2.1 ms.

  • 12 min read
  • Also on GitHub

Part 3 of 3 in WebGPU against WebGL2 · Previous: Scene depth for a post process: WebGPU against WebGL2

Question: a browser game built to read as a photograph can be given the look of a landscape in oils with two full-screen fragment shaders written once in GLSL: a stroke filter and a palette grade. On an engine that runs them on WebGPU as well as WebGL2, where do the two APIs part ways, and what does the same shader cost on each?

Short answer: the look is the same on both, and so is the cost once the shader has the right shape: the stroke filter, a An edge-preserving smoothing filter: it cuts the window round each pixel into sectors and keeps the mean of the flattest, so texture flattens into strokes while edges stay., added 2.3 ms a frame on each. Written the textbook way, its sector sums in arrays indexed by a loop variable, it cost 12.5 ms on WebGPU against 2.1 ms on WebGL2, because the longer translation chain to WebGPU's shading language. Babylon.js translates a GLSL shader to it through SPIR-V before the browser compiles it again for the GPU. kept the sums in memory. The other differences are smaller: loops that end on a uniform pass WGSL's uniformity analysis, a palette's uniforms become one padded buffer, and a frame rate read with the display's cap on can hide the filter's cost behind the cap.

In numbers:

  • 12.5 ms against 2.1 ms: the textbook filter's cost on WebGPU and on WebGL2, at 1200 by 675 with the display's cap off (section 2).
  • 2.3 ms on both for the same filter with its sums as named variables: a change of shape, not of arithmetic (section 2).
  • 60 fps is what WebGPU read with no filter until the cap was turned off, so a cost taken as a difference of frame rates can be wrong by the whole cap (section 2).
  • No diagnostic from WGSL's uniformity analysis for texture reads in loops that exit on uniforms; the one pass that branches per pixel turns the analysis off for itself alone (section 3).
  • 48 bytes for three vec3 tints in WebGPU's uniform buffer, where a tint and its amount packed as one vec4 take 16 bytes with no padding (section 4).
  • Three seconds to fade between named palettes (oils, ash, amber, none), for nothing beyond nine uniforms (section 7).

The worked example is Day Hike, a first-person hiking game in Babylon.js 9.18, public at game-dayhike and playable at games.csarko.sh/dayhike. As of Oct 17, 2026, its high tier draws with WebGPU in Chrome and Edge and everything else draws with WebGL2 (WebGPU vs WebGL2: what changes and what does not); the post chain's prepass for the far field is the subject of Scene depth for a post process. The machine throughout is an Apple M4 running Chrome 157, WebGPU through Metal and WebGL2 through ANGLE's Metal backend, with the render at 1200 by 675.

01

Strokes, a value curve and a palette, all in post

Three things, read off landscape painting in oils, and each a different part of the chain.

Strokes, not pixels. Fine texture is gone, replaced by strokes that keep the edges: bark is a few marks, grass a field of strokes, the sky a wash. The filter that does this is the Kuwahara filter and its descendants [1], [2], [3]: the window round each pixel is cut into sectors, each sector's mean and variance are taken, and the pixel becomes the means weighted by how flat each sector is. An edge is kept where the sectors on either side of it disagree, and the texture inside a region flattens into a stroke. The generalized form [2] weights each tap by a smooth function of where it lies in its sector; the anisotropic form [3] stretches the sectors along the image's structure so strokes follow bark and grass. What follows uses the generalized form with round sectors.

Value over hue. The pictures live on a wide tonal range, deep warm darks against a luminous sky, with muted colour inside it. A grade after the tone map widens the lit values round middle grey and leaves the deep shadow and the brightest lights where the render put them.

A warm, drained palette. Shadows go to an umber, midtones to an olive, highlights to a cream, the whole drained of a tenth of its saturation and its blacks lifted a shade. This is a split-tone, three tints with their amounts, and it belongs to the game rather than to the shader: a record the game sets by name and fades between, so a place or an event can turn the atmosphere.

The chain, then: the stroke filter first on the scene's linear colour, the grade after it with the palette's nine uniforms, and the rest of the chain as it was. None of this touches a material or a light. In Day Hike the world was also moved a step toward the palette afterward (a drearier opening weather, a warmer ground haze, the cover's green toward a sage, the sky's aerosols up for a paler noon), which is ordinary tuning and the same on both APIs.

02

Sector arrays cost 12.5 ms on WebGPU and 2.1 ms on WebGL2

The filter as first written was the textbook shape: one scan of the window, and at each tap a loop over the eight sectors adding the tap into that sector's mean, square and weight, the three held in function-scope arrays indexed by the sector. It compiles on both engines and draws the same picture on both. It does not cost the same.

Engine, medium tier, 1200 by 675, display cap off No stroke filter Sector arrays Named sums
WebGL2 (ANGLE, Metal) 55.3 fps, 18.1 ms 49.6 fps, 20.2 ms 49.1 fps, 20.4 ms
WebGPU (Dawn, Metal) 76.9 fps, 13.0 ms 39.3 fps, 25.5 ms 65.5 fps, 15.3 ms

The stroke radius is 2 px on this tier, 25 taps a window, and the frames were counted over 15 s with the display's cap off in Chrome (--disable-frame-rate-limit --disable-gpu-vsync), without which the WebGPU column reads 60 with no filter and the filter's cost cannot be told from the cap [4]. The array shape costs 2.1 ms on WebGL2 and 12.5 ms on WebGPU. The same filter with its sums as named variables, a function called eight times a tap with the sector's direction a constant and the window's loops run to the radius rather than to a fixed reach, costs 2.3 ms on both. A third shape tried between them, the sectors taken one after another with their own scan of the window each, no array but eight times the texture reads, cost more than the arrays on WebGL2 and about the same on WebGPU: the reads, not the arrays, were its cost.

What the two paths do with the same source explains it. On WebGL2, ANGLE translates GLSL ES 3.00 to Metal directly [5]. On WebGPU, Babylon.js translates GLSL to SPIR-V with glslang and the SPIR-V to WGSL with Tint, and the browser's Dawn compiles the WGSL to Metal again [6], [7]. A function-scope array indexed by a loop variable is, in WGSL, a variable in the function address space whose elements are addressed at run time [8]; the compiler at the end of the longer chain did not keep the eight accumulators in registers, and every tap's eight updates went through memory. The shorter chain did keep them. Writing the filter with no array to index is a change of shape and not of arithmetic, and it is the shape to write first on any shader that will run through a translation: the index that a compiler can see through on one path is one it may not on the other.

03

Loops that end on a uniform pass WGSL's uniformity analysis

The filter's window is a loop whose bound is the radius, a uniform, with an early exit inside it: a tap past the radius's circle is skipped with continue. GLSL ES 3.00 allows loops with any bound and any exit [9]. WGSL allows them too, and translates them; what WGSL adds is the uniformity analysis, which refuses a textureSample that it cannot prove is reached by every invocation of a quad together [10]. The previous article met the refusal: a texture read after a branch on a per-pixel value, fixed by reading an explicit level instead [11]. The filter's reads are inside loops whose exits depend on the loop counters, on the radius uniform and on the tap's place in its sector, none of them per-pixel, so the analysis accepts them as written, and the pass compiles with no diagnostic. The filter's reads are also made at an explicit level, which is the other way to satisfy the analysis and costs nothing on a target with one level.

The one pass in the chain that reads the scene under a per-pixel branch, the film finish, whose peripheral overlap reads the scene a second time inside a branch on the pixel's distance from the frame's centre, turns the analysis off for itself: the engine honours a per-shader define, DISABLE_UNIFORMITY_ANALYSIS, which it turns into WGSL's diagnostic(off, derivative_uniformity) for that one translation [12], [7]. It is safe there because the target has one mip level, so the level of detail an implicit-derivative read gives up cannot select another; it would not be safe on a mipmapped texture, where the read's derivatives are undefined on both APIs [9]. The engine also has the same switch page-wide, for every shader it translates, and that is the wrong tool on a renderer with hundreds of stages: the analysis is the one place that says which reads are the unsafe ones. Day Hike leaves the page-wide switch on, lets one stage turn the analysis off, and makes both the page-wide state and each stage's own switch part of the key under which its precompiled WGSL is looked up, so a translation made under one setting is never served under the other.

04

Nine uniforms: nine calls on WebGL2, one padded buffer on WebGPU

The palette is nine uniforms: three tints as vec3, three amounts as vec2, a saturation, a curve as vec2, a lift as vec3. The engine's post process takes a list of uniform names and a callback that sets them each frame, and that is the whole of the API on both engines. Underneath it is not the same object. On WebGL2 each name is a uniform location and each set is a call [13]. On WebGPU a post process's uniforms are one uniform buffer, and WGSL lays a buffer out with alignment rules: a vec3 of f32 is 12 bytes in size but aligned to 16, so three tints as vec3 are padded to 48 bytes, and a vec2 is aligned to 8 [14]. The engine writes the buffer with those rules, and nothing in the shader changes. What the rules decide is how a palette should be declared when it grows: a tint and its amount as one vec4 each (the three channels and the density, the saturation beside the next) is 16 bytes a band with no padding and one set a band, where vec3 and vec2 apart are 32 bytes and two sets. The nine uniforms here are set once a frame and cost nothing either way; a palette set a draw, or a hundred of them, would be packed.

05

Half-float targets and full-screen passes work the same on both

The look is the same on both engines because all of it is fragment work on full-screen passes, which both APIs have in the same shape: a half-float render target, a sampler, a quad. The chain runs on linear colour in a half-float render target, so the stroke filter sees the scene's dynamic range and the tone map in the grade sees the filter's output as the renderer made it. A half-float target is renderable on WebGL2 through EXT_color_buffer_float and on WebGPU as the rgba16float format [15], [16]; the engine asks for the one it has and the chain is built on it. The first pass of the chain owns the scene's render target and carries its multisampling, a blit to resolve on one API and a resolve target on the other, which the previous article covered [11]. Where the stroke filter is first in the chain it takes that place; where the far field's prepass is first, the filter follows it and reads its output. Nothing here differed between the engines beyond what the earlier article found.

06

Strokes on linear light turn leaves cyan, on both APIs

The trap that took longest was the same on both engines. The stroke filter was written on the scene's linear colour, before the tone map, and at noon it painted. At a low sun it turned every crown against the sky cyan-white, with holes where leaves had been, on both engines alike. Two things compound. A sun seen through a crown is a texel of ten or a hundred where the leaves beside it are tenths; in a sector's mean and variance the one texel owns the sector, and the flattest sector near a crown is the sky's. And a leaf seen against a bright sky takes the sky's sector, because it is the flattest, and vanishes into it.

The first fix, taking the statistics on the colour compressed toward one (c over 1 + c) and expanding the chosen mean again, made it worse in a way worth recording: a mean taken in the compressed range and expanded is not a mean of the colours, and near the top of the range it bends toward whichever channel was brighter, so a salmon leaf beside a pale sky came back cyan. The fix that held judges the sectors on the compressed colour, so the bright texel is one among the others, and writes the sectors' mean of the linear colour itself, capped, so the colour written is a colour the scene had. A sector whose mean is far from the pixel's own colour weighs less, so a thin leaf keeps its own sector against a flat sky. With that the crowns stood at every hour.

The grade's value curve met a cousin of the same thing: widening the lit values round middle grey pushed the brightest leaf past one and it clipped channel by channel to the same cyan. The fix rolls the curve off above the upper midtones and leaves the deep shadow alone from the other side, so a dark evening stays legible.

07

The palette is a record the game fades between

Because the split-tone is a record and not a constant, the frame's tonal identity is a thing the game can set: the default oils; an ash, cold and drained, for a dead place; an amber, an evening gone to gold; and none, the render as the tone map leaves it. A palette is set by name and the frame fades from the one it wears to the new one over three seconds, every value of the record interpolated. This costs nothing on either engine beyond the nine uniforms, and it is the part of the work that a game can build on: a weather that carries its own palette, a place that fades one in as the player enters it, a palette as the sign that something has changed.

08

Not measured: a discrete GPU and the anisotropic filter

The frame rates are from one machine, through two translation chains, at one resolution; the rig pairs on a discrete GPU, where the array shape may well cost what it should, were not taken. The anisotropic form of the filter, whose sectors follow the image's structure tensor, is the one the paper recommends and costs a pass more to build the tensor, a fragment pass to a texture on WebGL2 or either that or a compute pass on WebGPU; its cost on each was not measured. And the uniformity diagnostic the film finish still logs was read, not removed.
