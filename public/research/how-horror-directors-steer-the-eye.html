Five Ways Horror Directors Steer the Eye

Horror inverts five framing rules: space behind the face, threat in the dark, focus on nothing, lines to nowhere. The eye is measured; the dread is lore.

  • 42 min read

Part 6 of 7 in Horror craft · Next: How Horror Directors Make Sound Carry the Fear

Question: General composition primers teach five devices as rules: give a character lead room, let brightness and contrast draw the eye, use focus and the A focus pull during the shot that moves the sharp plane from one subject to another, telling the viewer what matters at that instant. to say what matters, run leading lines to the subject, and keep the frame balanced. How do renowned horror directors and their cinematographers use or invert those five devices, including Framing a face against the edge of the frame it looks toward, so the empty space sits behind the head instead of in front of the gaze. and the A shot in which the camera is rolled so vertical lines lean, also called a Dutch angle or Dutch tilt., to steer where the audience looks and to convey dread, threat, isolation and a world gone wrong? And which of the claimed effects are measured rather than craft lore?

Short answer: Horror treats each rule as a promise to the viewer and breaks the promise on purpose. Longlegs and Halloween leave the empty space behind or beside the character so that it reads as a place a threat can enter; The Thing, Nosferatu (2024) and Us put the brightest surface behind the figure so the eye gets a shape without a face; The Invisible Man and Ringu put the sharp plane where nothing visible is, while It Follows refuses to pull focus at all; The Shining and Suspiria run converging lines to an empty or impossible end; Smile and Midsommar roll the horizon when the protagonist's world turns over. The laboratory has measured the defaults these moves violate, centre bias, the inward-facing bias, sharpness and contrast as gaze attractors, and the attentional synchrony that mainstream editing produces, but as of Oct 23, 2026 no study has measured dread, threat or arousal from any of the five inversions, so the feeling side rests on craft testimony that is strikingly consistent across sixty years of makers.

In short:

  • "Fear lives behind your back": Longlegs cinematographer Andrés Arochi leaves blank space where the character is not looking, the exact inverse of the lead-room rule, and Dean Cundey calls the same region in Halloween "lurking space" (section 2)
  • Three DPs, one operation: Zach Kuperstein, Larkin Seiple and Radek Ladczuk each describe adding one highlight or one black so the rest of the frame can go dark and the eye has a single anchor (section 3)
  • "Often out of focus": Hideo Nakata states the J-horror ghost principle outright, Stefan Duscio pushes focus "past a foreground character into an unlikely area of the frame," and It Follows hands the scanning to the audience with wide lenses and deep focus (section 4)
  • A 9.8mm lens 24 inches off the floor: Garrett Brown's Steadicam and Kubrick's wall-to-wall corridors produce The Shining's converging lines; the menace reading is the critics', not Kubrick's (section 5)
  • Three flips in Smile, one roll in Midsommar: the canted or inverted frame is used sparingly, and its only maker explanation on record is "the world turned upside down to reflect Rose's state of mind" (section 6)
  • Zero studies of dread: centre bias, inward bias, sharpness and contrast attraction and attentional synchrony are measured; no peer-reviewed study tests fear from short-siding, silhouette, blur, an empty vanishing point or a Dutch tilt (section 8)

01

The defaults horror exploits: centre, faces, motion, contrast, sharpness

The five rules are stated the same way across teaching texts. Lead room "is the space in front, and in the direction of, moving or stationary subjects," and without it a moving subject's "progress will seem impeded" [1]; working cinematographer Neil Oseman gives the practical form, "If a character is looking screen-left, certainly it's most common to place them on the right of frame," and adds that it is "a rule that's made to be broken" [2]. On light, Jay Holben's ASC Shot Craft column says "the key to keeping things visually interesting is in the mix of highlights and shadow" and warns that "An overall underexposed image is hard on the eye and tends to look muddy" [3]. On focus, David Bordwell holds up the "in-your-face deep focus" of Panic in the Streets and asks directors to "back off, in other words, from intensified continuity and go for something more daring and original," intensified continuity being his name for the contemporary style of closer framings, faster cutting and focus pulls [4]. On lines, StudioBinder says leading lines are "one technique filmmakers use to get the audience focus on what they want them to focus on" [5]. On balance, the root text is Rudolf Arnheim's: as Hübner and Fillinger summarise it, "each rectangular frame has a hidden structure or field of invisible forces," the centre pulls hardest, weight rises with size and with distance from the centre, and a picture looks balanced when the weights compensate one another [6].

What the eye does before any rule is applied has been measured, mostly on still pictures and on non-horror film. Palmer, Gardner and Wickens found, using forced choice, adjustment and free photography, that "preference was greatest for pictures whose subject was located at or near the center of the frame" and, for sideways-facing objects, "an additional preference for objects to face into rather than out of the frame (the inward bias)" [7]. In moving images, Smith and Mital report that initial saccades default toward the screen centre, high motion and people before task control takes over [8], and Mital and colleagues found that flicker and motion predicted where gaze clustered better than static features such as corners and orientations [9]. Itti and Koch's saliency model formalises the bottom-up pull of conspicuous locations and notes that the same object may or may not be salient depending on what surrounds it [10]. Smith and Tadmor found that observers fixate sharp or lightly blurred regions first and blurrier regions later or not at all, even when contrast is matched [11]. Loschky and colleagues, tracking viewers of a Touch of Evil excerpt, report that typical Hollywood films "induce strong attentional synchrony," that "most viewers look at the same things at the same time," and that their most robust result was "the overwhelming attentional synchrony created by the intensified continuity editing style," whose tools they list as "manipulation of motion, luminance contrast, image framing" [12].

Set the two lists side by side and the pattern of this report appears. The rules codify measured defaults: the eye wants the subject near the centre and facing in, goes to the brightest contrast edge, lands on the sharp plane, follows converging lines and reads a balanced frame as settled. Horror's cinematographers use those defaults in two ways, to send the eye to one place so that the threat can sit unobserved in another, and to withhold the default so that the frame feels wrong before anything in it does. The eye is still steered; it is steered toward an absence.

Headroom, negative space in general, depth staging (victim in the foreground, threat in the background), the frame within a frame and symmetry are covered shot by shot in the two companion reports, Horror cinematography shot by shot and Horror sequences shot by shot. This report points to them where a device borders theirs and does not restate them.

02

Lead room: horror puts the empty space behind the character

Lead room is a promise about the next cut. Bordwell, writing on widescreen framing, explains the contract: "close views in widescreen typically leave a lot of dead space," an off-centre figure "can suggest that the object of attention is somewhere beyond the empty zone," and in shot/reverse shot "the empty area of the first shot is filled by the corresponding figure or action in the next shot" [13]. Short-siding breaks the promise: the face is pushed against the edge it looks toward, the empty space sits behind the head, and the next shot never fills it.

The craft definition comes from outside horror

As of Oct 23, 2026 no horror cinematographer has been found using the word "short-siding" about a scene in their own film, so the clearest maker statement of what it does remains Tod Campbell's on Mr. Robot (USA Network, 2015 to 2019, creator Sam Esmail), cited here as the craft reference and not as a horror example. Vulture's Sean T. Collins glossed the convention as "leaving what's known as 'leading room' between their faces that helps convey the physical space they occupy," then quoted Campbell: "Shortsighting is unnerving," and "The idea was to convey the loneliness" [14]. Campbell also described reserving the conventional frame as a payoff: "We'd save putting him either on what you'd call the 'correct' side of the frame in a conversation or in the center, and really reserve those moments for something where we felt the subtext would be more profound" [14]. Two years later he put the entrapment reading in terms any horror DP would recognise: "putting him at the bottom of the corner of the frame makes it so there's no place for him to go" [15]. Critic Jason Haggstrom's definition is the one teaching sites repeat: the character looks "towards the edge of the frame that they are most closely positioned rather than across the length of the frame to where their partner in conversation will appear after the next cut" [16], and Oseman reads such subjects as "unbalanced, lost, trapped, wrong-footed, or isolated" [2].

Horror DPs describe the same move from the other end

Horror cinematographers rarely talk about where the face goes; they talk about where the empty space goes, and they want it where the character is not looking. The cleanest statement is Andrés Arochi's on Longlegs (2024, director Osgood Perkins), given to Filmmaker Magazine and read here on Deep Fried Movies' attributed repost: "I always wanted to leave blank space in the frame because, for me, fear lives behind your back. It's not in front of you. If you're working on your computer at 1 a.m. and you feel something, it's always behind you. So, it was this idea of always having space for fear to live" [17]. That is lead room inverted as a principle: the void belongs to the direction the character does not face. Arochi noticed that "Maika as Agent Lee Harker barely moves," gave her "a wider lens," and decided "Even if she's in a room surrounded by people, let's abstract her" [17].

Dean Cundey described the same principle on Halloween (1978, director John Carpenter) in the same anamorphic frame. He and Carpenter discussed in prep "the visual mechanics of scaring the audience," meaning red herrings, double scares and misdirection, and "organized them as a set of techniques"; the format mattered because "One of the great things about the anamorphic frame is that there's a lot you can do with the edges of compositions," creating "'lurking space' of shadows around the subject, like in darkened doorways" "where something might or might not be hiding," which is "psychologically stressful and create[s] tension" [18]. Cundey adds that audiences have learned this grammar, "so it's a matter of figuring out how to twist that a little bit" [18]. The critics' reading of how this plays in Halloween is Fangoria's: Laurie walking to and from school is framed with "a copious amount of space on the sides of her," the payoff is Michael "stepping into frame and lurking near Laurie," and the open space becomes "a potential threat that can be anywhere in this wide frame" [19]. No Carpenter or Cundey quote describes those particular shots.

Charlie Sarroff on Smile (2022, director Parker Finn) states both halves of the inversion. The tight half: "We wanted everything to feel claustrophobic. Some of the eye lines were very tight to frame." The empty half: "We wanted her to feel vulnerable and lonely. Empty spaces with Rose centered up close." And the principle: "what you don't see or can't see is as important as what you do see," because "It's often just a question of moving a little bit to the left or right" [20]. Mike Gioulakis says the same of It Follows (2014, director David Robert Mitchell): the tension came from "seeing the approaching danger in the background and having that in a wide field of view" [21]. Kiyoshi Kurosawa, quoted by Virginie Sélavy, wants to "express (…) the world that lies beyond what is visible on-screen"; her gloss is that he places us "in a position from which we are able to see shadows move and shapes appear in the background" [22].

What the record does not support

Two pieces of lore circulate without a maker behind them. The first is the fleeing character framed hard against the leading edge so the chase reads as blocked; the nearest documented statements are the textbook mechanism, that starved lead room makes progress "seem impeded" [1], and Campbell's static "no place for him to go" [15]. The second is short-siding as a sign of dishonesty; the documented readings are isolation and a world that is wrong (Campbell) and "apprehension and conflict" in Haggstrom's account of Fincher and Refn [16], and dishonesty appears only in unsourced glossaries. The counter-strategy is Robert Eggers and Jarin Blaschke's centre-weighted horror, whose lenses were chosen because their vignetting "draws your eye to the center, which works well for symmetrical compositions" [23]; that mode is the subject of the companion report on symmetry and the frame within a frame.

Measured: the rule is a real preference, the dread is not measured

The inward bias is among the best-replicated findings in empirical aesthetics. Palmer and colleagues found it in the laboratory alongside a centre bias that fell off "monotonically and symmetrically with distance from the center" [7], and Bertamini, Bennett and Bode found it in centuries of animal portraiture: "there was more space in front of the animals compared to the space behind the animal. This is a case of Inward bias as defined by Palmer et al. (2008)" [24]. Short-siding therefore violates a measured preference, which is consistent with Campbell's "unnerving" and Oseman's "wrong-footed." But no study has measured fear, threat or arousal from short-sided versus conventional faces in moving images, and no eye-tracking study of lead room exists. Since gaze in film clusters on people [8], the "lurking space" beside a face is probably not where the eye rests; whatever it does, it does through peripheral expectation, and that is an inference, not a tested result.

Takeaway: Lead room is a promise that the empty side will be filled by the next shot. Horror keeps the empty side and refuses to fill it, and three generations of DPs (Cundey, Sarroff, Arochi) describe the void behind or beside the character as the place a threat lives. The measured preference for inward-facing, centred subjects explains why the frame feels wrong; nothing has measured the fear.

03

Brightness: the eye goes to the light, the dread sits in the dark

The rule and its inverse, in the makers' words

The ASC's craft writing states rule and inversion in one breath. Stephen Pizzello lists the horror toolkit as "Strategically directional lighting, silhouettes, spotlighting, underexposure, projected shadows" [25]. In the same piece Roy H. Wagner, ASC, gives the inverse of the brightness rule as a horror principle: "In a movie, where the shadows are is where the audience doesn't want to be," and "surround that person with shadows, the audience instinctively wants to know what's going on in that darkness"; of The Innocents (1961, DP Freddie Francis), "They allow you to see the whole frame, but they only show you what they want you to see" [25]. Holben supplies the mechanics: "Don't be afraid to have overexposed elements in dark scenes," because small bright accents keep the viewer's pupils closed so blacks read crisper, and "If someone is lurking in the dark and picks up a knife, it may not be necessary to see their face … A selective pool of light right at the hands will do" [3].

The oldest maker statement in this report is producer Val Lewton's. A 1944 press release quoted by George E. Turner in American Cinematographer promised that "audiences will people any patch of prepared darkness with more horror, suspense and frightfulness" [26]; the better-known version, relayed through J. P. Telotte's study, is "If you make the screen dark enough, the mind's eye will read anything into it you want! We're great ones for dark patches" [27]. Robert Wise carried the method into The Haunting (1963, DP Davis Boulton): "I wanted the hallways to look long and dark, so I asked [Panavision's Bob Gottschalk] if he didn't have a wider lens," and "The style took me back to my roots with Val"; Turner describes the payoff as "the camera holds on the wall as the moonlight slowly shifts, revealing a scowling face hidden within the decor" [26]. A luminance change, not a cut or a focus pull, makes a latent figure legible.

Cundey and Carpenter: the reveal is a lighting effect

Halloween's climactic doorway reveal, Michael's mask materialising in the dark beside Laurie, is often described as a focus trick. It is not. The shot is "the famous hallway scene where the hidden face of Michael Myers is slowly revealed by way of a blue light next to the mask" [28]: the figure is in frame and in focus throughout, and visibility, not sharpness, is withheld. Cundey told Den of Geek that Carpenter "was not afraid of having dark areas in the frame because he understood the importance," and that "creating the illusion of dark" is "one of the greatest challenges" a cinematographer faces [29]. On The Thing (1982) the silhouette solved a practical problem: lighting the creature directly "could give away the fact that it was a lump of plastic with paint on it," so "I would light the back wall of the set so that you could see the shape of the creature," in "a very interesting game of showing just enough for the audience to understand what was happening" [18].

Roizman's Exorcist: backlit breath and a bright window

Owen Roizman's 1974 account of The Exorcist (1973, director William Friedkin) is the most detailed exposure testimony in the record. The bedroom was refrigerated so breath would show, and "The challenge was to get backlight on the breath while keeping everything else dark"; "What we tried to do, by means of the lighting, was to give it a kind of ominous feeling" [30]. Merrin's arrival, the poster shot, is the brightness rule in pure form: the camera looks "down a deserted, foggy street," headlights emerge, and "The priest gets out and stands in the bright glow coming from the little girl's bedroom window" [30]. The window is the brightest area, so the eye goes there; the priest is a silhouette in mist and lamplight; the dark house mass is where the audience knows the threat to be. Friedkin's model, as Artnet reports from his remarks in A Decade Under the Influence, was Magritte's Empire of Light, a day sky over a dark house with one lit lamp [31]. Wagner's gloss on the palette is the colour form of the rule: "that cold, green-blue color immediately tells you it's not safe in there" [25].

Blaschke: one source, fast fall-off, every way to obscure

Jarin Blaschke's method across three Eggers films is single-source naturalism with steep fall-off, which makes the one practical the only bright area and pushes everything behind the subject to black. On The Lighthouse (2019): "if there's a lantern on the table, I just put a lantern on the table"; "it was just a bare bulb, and I would net it off, so it would fall off even faster" [32]. On Nosferatu (2024) the silhouette is one item in a deliberate inventory: "All the ways you could obscure this person, we used them all! Silhouette with the lights behind him"; the reveal gives "Orlok's back and his hands," then "a shot of his eyes, so you don't see the rest" [33]. CineD describes the fireplace shot as Orlok "a mere silhouette against the flames" [34]: the fire is the brightest region and sits behind the figure, so the eye takes the outline and is denied the face.

Gioulakis, Hereditary and the three-DP operation

Gioulakis on Us (2019, director Jordan Peele) explains the driveway silhouette of the Tethered as a preference: "I have an aversion to moonlight, at least in movies," so "we decided that having a street lamp behind these characters" would carry the scene, and the exposure target was "just enough of the character so you can read the expression, but you don't want it to feel too lit up" [35]. Hereditary's most discussed hidden figure, Annie on the ceiling above Peter's bed, is a darkness-and-placement effect: Creepy Catalog locates her "in the upper left-hand corner" of a dim frame and quotes psychologist Arryn Robbins on why viewers miss her, "You pull detailed information only from wherever your attention is pointed" [36]. No maker statement on the shot exists and nothing says it was shot soft; the eye is held on the lit, central, moving Peter while the threat sits dark at the edge.

Three contemporary DPs describe one operation from both ends. Kuperstein on Barbarian (2022): "it's often about keeping something bright in the background so you can make the rest of the image dark" [37]. Seiple on Weapons: "if you're going to go dark, make sure you have things in the background to reflect or to create silhouettes against," because "the first thing that audiences look at when they look at an image is the actor's eyes," and at the limit, "I always felt like when it gets too dark, it's telling the audience to listen" [38]. Ladczuk on The Babadook (2014, director Jennifer Kent) adds the black instead of the highlight: "We tried to add black elements in every frame just to create contrast," and the light hardened through the film "because the light was motivated by fear" [39]. Sinister (2012) made the black physical: DP Chris Norr recalls "putting black on the ceiling, putting black everywhere," on a Caravaggio model [40]. Blacked-out ceilings are Lewton's prepared darkness in plywood, and the shared rule is that darkness without a highlight reads as a fault rather than a choice.

Measured: contrast attracts, but motion attracts more

Saliency models support contrast as a real bottom-up attractor [10]. Loschky and colleagues note that films have increased "contrast between dark and light regions," shortened shots and increased motion, features that "strongly attract attention" [12], and Loschky says the medium is "amazing at directing the viewers' attention from moment to moment in exactly the way that the filmmaker wants" [41]. But in moving pictures the strongest measured predictor of gaze is motion and flicker, with luminance contrast weaker [9]. In a static dark frame, contrast is the dominant cue, which is where Lewton's and Cundey's dark patch works; add motion in the dark region and the eye goes there regardless of brightness, which is the It Follows strategy in section 4.

Takeaway: Every brightness statement in the record is two-sided. The bright area is where the eye is sent (Holben, Seiple, Kuperstein); the dark area is where the audience is made to fear looking (Lewton, Wagner, Cundey). The silhouette is the hinge: a lit background gives the category of the threat without its face, and Cundey's "lump of plastic" and Blaschke's "every way to obscure" are the same rationale forty years apart.

04

Focus: the soft shape, the misdirected pull and the deep-focus counter-school

The rule and horror's three answers

In the intensified-continuity style the rack focus is the standard way to move attention between planes, and Bordwell's plea is to abandon it for depth staging [4]. A 2026 shot-design essay on the AMC series Interview with the Vampire states the rule bluntly, that the most literal use of a rack is to tell the audience where to look at that instant, and adds that racks can "make us feel how hard they are concentrating on their conversation partner" [42]. Horror has three answers: make the threat soft and keep it soft, put the sharp plane on nothing, or refuse to rack at all.

The soft shape: Nakata states the principle

Hideo Nakata's sentence is the clearest maker statement of the soft-ghost principle: "Traditionally in J-horror, a ghost, something which isn't supposed to exist, appears faintly as an apparition behind you, often out of focus," with Ringu (1998, DP Junichiro Hayashi) as the instance, "when the TV gets turned off, you see her reflection on the blank screen, but when you turn around she's not there" [43]. The shape is sensed before it is identified, and the sharp plane stays on the living character so the viewer's eye is slow to confirm what the periphery has registered. Kurosawa's method is adjacent but different: his ghosts "just show up and sit in the corner of the room to tell you how vengeful they feel" [44], and Sarah Mae Fleming reads his distant framing as making viewers "acutely aware of the periphery" [45]. That is distance and stillness, not focus; when Film Comment raised an out-of-focus ghost face in Daguerrotype, Kurosawa replied, "This is the first time I'm hearing this insight" [46]. No statement by Takashi Shimizu on how Kayako was photographed in Ju-On, and none by Hayashi on Sadako, has been found online.

The misdirected pull: Duscio puts the sharp plane on nothing

Stefan Duscio, shooting The Invisible Man (2020, director Leigh Whannell), is the only cinematographer in the record who names focus as the misdirected instrument. The team "suggestively filmed empty spaces, letting the camera linger hauntingly on the mundane corners of a room"; "Focus might push past a foreground character into an unlikely area of the frame"; and "Some of these techniques might feel 'wrong' or unusually composed, but our aim was to create a relentless tension" [47]. To Australian Cinematographer he wanted to "suggest someone else could be inhabiting the negative space in the frame" [48]. Stillness was the enabling condition: "Leigh and I really wanted very still camera work," "so it'd encourage the audience to look around the frame"; Whannell's direction, as Duscio recalls it, was "I want compositions to feel incorrect" [49]. Meagan Navarro's critics' reading: "The audience subconsciously connects the emptiness of the room with the eponymous invisible foe stalking Cecilia" [50]. The sharp plane sits where the threat would be, so the viewer reads empty space as occupied.

Two famous "focus" scares are not focus scares. Halloween's doorway reveal is a lighting effect, as section 3 describes [28]. Hereditary's ceiling shot hides Annie with darkness and edge placement [36]; what Aster says of the film's gaze is "I just wanted to make a film that kind of locked eyes with the audience" and "there's this feeling of the family being surveilled" [51], and the one focus tool Pogorzelski documents is "tilt-shift wide (angle lenses) to make the actual house feel like it was part of the miniature" [52], which serves the dollhouse conceit, not the scares.

The deep-focus counter-school: It Follows hands the scanning to the viewer

The sharpest answer to the rack is to refuse it. Mitchell told Complex, "I wanted to shoot pretty wide-angled lenses, and I wanted to make sure that we could see far into the distance," and explained the game: "You would have some extras way off in the distance, and sometimes it would [be] people connected to the 'it' and sometimes not," because "We're not going to tell you every time that something dangerous is in the frame or is approaching," so "you sort of hand it off to the audience and put it on their shoulders to feel the need to look everywhere at all times" [53]. To Yahoo he added the hold and the honesty: "we'll hold on a cut a little bit longer than I think some people will be used to," and "We don't really cheat distance very often with the camera via longer lenses or a quick cut," because "it's about being honest with geography and distance" [54]. Gioulakis confirms the brief: "David wanted to film this with very deep focus" and a camera "that wasn't based on 'jump scares'" but "allowed the audience to see everything, presenting the scene as a tableau" [21]; the lighting was designed "so that your eye will be able to scan as much of the frame as possible" [55]. Wide field, deep field, long hold, no cheat: the viewer's own scanning is the suspense engine, and Mitchell names the model, "There was a bunch of John Carpenter stuff, for sure, like Halloween and The Fog" [53].

Two canonical films belong in this school rather than the shallow one. Rosemary's Baby (1968, director Roman Polanski, DP William Fraker) was photographed, apart from its opening shot, "with either a 18mm or 25mm lens"; Polanski wanted "depth, not just a flat field," because "things are hidden from your camera, you can discover something that was hidden when you move sideways" [56]. The Ruth Gordon phone shot, framed on Polanski's instruction so that only part of her shows past a door edge, produced in Fraker's words the reaction, "I saw everyone leaning in their seats, trying to look around the edge of the door frame to see what was happening" [56]. The leaning-in reflex is triggered by occlusion and depth, with no blur at all. The Shining (1980) was likewise deep by design: John Alcott "could work at a T/2.8 to T/4 aperture most of the time and that gave us sufficient depth," and in the maze "I was usually stopped down to T/5.6, and even sometimes T/8" because "We wanted depth of field" [57].

Found footage and the ungradable image

Found footage inverts focus from the other direction: the whole frame sits below the identification threshold, so the viewer searches noise. Skinamarink (2022, director Kyle Edward Ball, DP Jamie McRae) is the limit case, and Ball separates intent from accident: "I always wanted to have grain," but "in the grain you see shapes that maybe are there, that was completely an accident"; the withholding was deliberate, "we never see anyone's faces and the grown-ups even more," so "we have to fill in blanks either consciously or subconsciously" [58]. His script direction was "We hold on this shot of a hallway for 45 seconds" [59]. Ball's admission that the pareidolia was accidental matters: the effect belongs to the viewer's visual system, not to authorial intent.

Measured: sharpness pulls, blur does not push

Smith and Tadmor's composites found sharp regions fixated first and blurrier regions later or not at all, with the pattern holding when contrast was matched [11]. The Kansas State Visual Cognition Lab summarises its own work: "there is a threshold level of image resolution below which the eyes are less likely to go to a certain image region," and the Peterson studies conclude that "unique clarity captures attention, while unique blur is ignored by selective attention," rejecting the hypothesis that blur repels [60]. Three consequences follow. A soft background ghost is not pushed out of attention; it fails to win against the sharp foreground until motion, luminance or a rack tips the balance, so moving the sharp plane, as Duscio does, is moving the attractor. The resolution threshold gives a mechanism for the found-footage effect: when the whole frame is below threshold there is no attractor and gaze wanders, which viewers experience as searching. And no study has eye-tracked a moving rack focus, none has used horror stimuli, and no focus-puller's account of a horror pull has been found online, so rack timing is documented only as director and DP intent.

Takeaway: Horror's three answers to "focus says look here" are Nakata's soft ghost (keep the threat below the sharpness that attracts), Duscio's misdirected plane (make the sharp region empty so the void reads as occupied) and Mitchell's refusal (deep focus, long hold, honest geography, so the audience must scan). The measured result that blur is ignored rather than repelled explains why the first two work and why the third is the most demanding.

05

Leading lines: corridors and stairs that converge on nothing

The Shining: the lines are a construction fact, the menace is a reading

The Overlook's corridors are the most cited one-point perspective in horror, and the primary accounts of how they were made are mechanical. Garrett Brown, in American Cinematographer in 1980 (read on Stabilizer News' reprint), describes the sets as "a maze; absurdly oversized quarters for the players, yet ultimately claustrophobic," built with the Steadicam in mind; for the Big Wheel shots the arm was mounted on a wheelchair so the lens could run about one inch off the floor; the maze was shot with a 9.8mm Kinoptik at about 24 inches and "could not have been photographed as Kubrick intended by any other means" [61]. Brown's own account of the viewer effect is that the Steadicam "puts the viewer right into the middle of the action" and could "lurk in the shadows with a menacing presence" [61]. Alcott confirms the construction logic: "The hotel was actually built wall-to-wall," and "one could only travel through it the way we did by using the Steadicam" [57]. Neither man mentions vanishing points.

The meaning is critics' work. Slate's account of kogonada's video essay defines one-point perspective as "compositions in which spatial planes converge at one vanishing point in the distance" and notes that "Kubrick also tended to place his subjects right over that vanishing point, dead-center in the frame" [62]. Michael Mariani reads the tricycle sequence as one in which "the viewer's line of sight is inexorably drawn to the center of the shot" as the walls "converge on a single point at the end of each corridor," an "unfamiliar, perhaps uncanny vantage" with "a visually disturbing potency" [63]. The disruption half is also critical: the Idyllopus Press spatial analysis argues the Overlook's parts "don't connect" as the cutting implies, that the Gold Room "would be sitting out in the parking lot," and that Kubrick "guides one to think it does, and treats it all very normally," so that "cognitive dissonance is quelled but never absolutely quieted" [64]. Order at shot scale, disorder at building scale. The Gold Room bathroom scene, where Kubrick crosses the axis so Jack and Grady swap sides, is described by No Film School as a break that "create[s] a jarring cut that reinforces the uneasiness of the scene" [65]; the symmetry baseline that makes such a break register, which ProVideo Coalition links to viewers who "tend to expect something to happen" [66], belongs to the companion report.

Caligari bends the lines; Nosferatu leaves them straight

The two poles of line disruption were set in 1920 and 1922. In The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (director Robert Wiene; designers Hermann Warm, Walter Reimann, Walter Röhrig), as R. Barton Palmer describes it, "streets cut across one another at impossible angles and paths are impossibly steep," and the governing idea is Warm's manifesto that "the cinema image must become an engraving" [67]. The skewed line stands for the inner state, and the oblique set, not a rolled camera, is where the Expressionist imbalance lives (section 6). Nosferatu (1922, director F. W. Murnau) is the counter-case: Gilberto Perez Guillermo notes that the town is "unblinkingly photographed in deep focus and natural light," that the vampire is often held at a distance "across an archway in his castle," and that Murnau's compositions have "a certain imbalance, a deliberate incompleteness" [68]. The lines-as-threat device, a staircase the shadow climbs or an archway the figure stands beyond, works inside realist space; it needs no distortion.

Suspiria: Escher on the walls

Suspiria's architecture is Caligari in colour, and the evidence is critics' observation, since Tovoli's remarks are about colour and sharpness: his aim was "a total abstraction from what we call 'everyday reality,'" with the audience "strangely attracted to proceed deeper and deeper into this colorful journey" [69]. John Coulthart identifies Madame Blanc's office mural as "a strange confection of Escher motifs, all winding staircases and architecture borrowed from Belvedere (1958)" [70], and Dan Lybarger adds that designer Giuseppe Bassan "designs walls that face each other in ways they would never do in real life" [71]. The impossible staircases are literally on the wall, and Tovoli's "deeper and deeper" describes a pull toward the vanishing point even though he credits it to colour.

Hereditary's dollhouse and Eggers's vertical frames

Ari Aster gives the clearest maker statement of lines used to shrink people. The Grahams are "a set of dolls in a maligned dollhouse, manipulated by outside forces"; the interiors "were all built on a stage" so that walls could be removed and the camera placed "at a great height" with wide lenses; "I like my wide shots to be very wide" [72]. Pogorzelski's version is that the widening lenses were there "to give the house its own character and make the people in it feel like dolls trapped inside" [73]. The cutaway is a leading-lines device in reverse: the stage walls go, the receding lines of floor and ceiling run unbroken to the far room, and the family sits at the small end of them. Eggers and Blaschke chose line direction at the format level: on The Witch's 1.66:1, "We wanted to be really intimate in the family's house, but also have the trees be extra tall" [23], and on The Lighthouse's 1.19:1 the ratio "frames the vertical lighthouse even better, isolates our two characters even more" "and truly traps them together when they do share a frame" [32].

Stairs: the one line makers explain in craft terms

Stairs are the line horror makers consistently explain, and they explain them as a figure moving through space, not as symbols. Hitchcock told Roger Ebert that staircases are built to be climbed and descended, "And therefore, they become very photogenic," because they lift a figure through space "instead of keeping a figure on the flat" [74]. Roizman calls the Georgetown steps of The Exorcist "a long narrow chute of steps and lighting that was a big number, also" [30]. Kuperstein on Barbarian: of the 15mm on the stairs, "It was really nice to have low distortion and the clean lines of the stairs going up"; the house's two worlds got two grammars, "The upstairs should feel like a David Fincher movie, and the downstairs should feel like a Sam Raimi movie"; and the tunnels were "lit only with flashlights" [75]. The up-equals-attic, down-equals-cellar meanings are critics' gloss, and no maker statement has been found online for the Ju-On staircase crawl, the Ringu well or Psycho's Arbogast staircase beyond process descriptions.

Corridors and roads: the line is given to the viewer

Ridley Scott describes wanting the Nostromo's corridors as free space, first trying "lighting everywhere at once, so that I would have total freedom of movement through the corridors" before reverting with Derek Vanlint to lighting "very directional and, for the sake of the mood of the story, rather low-key" [76]. Arochi's "space for fear to live" and Cundey's "lurking space" are the corridor principle stated forty-six years apart: leave an area the eye is drawn to check and that stays unresolved. The road is the line given most openly. Daniel Pearl on The Texas Chain Saw Massacre (1974, director Tobe Hooper) describes the underslung-dolly approach, "The house will start out small in the frame, and as we come closer, it will grow and grow," under a rule of "shots moving with a point" [77]. It Follows gives the street to the audience for the reason Mitchell gave them deep focus: so they can watch the thing travel down it [21].

Measured: convergence attracts gaze, with one caveat

The eye-tracking record supports the first half of the rule with an important correction. Borji and Feng's 2015 arXiv paper reported fixations clustering at the vanishing point far above chance, but the paper was withdrawn by its author, whose arXiv comment reads, "There is a major problem with the arguments and results unfortunately," so its figures should not be relied on [78]. The intact result is the earlier model paper by Borji, Feng and Lu, published at WACV 2016, which adds a vanishing-point channel to existing saliency models, improves fixation prediction by roughly 4.6 to 7.2 percent in AUC, and concludes that "vanishing point is an strong predictor of eye movements in free viewing" [79]. The second half, that lines hold attention when something sits at their end, has a 2024 study by Chuang, Tseng and Chiang: 34 participants viewed 15 leading-line photographs and 15 matched versions with the subject removed, and "leading lines significantly influenced the participants' attention to key elements of the work," strongest "when prominent subjects were present," with subjects producing "considerably reduced saccadic movements, and increased fixation duration" [80]. The design has no no-line control. Read for horror, it suggests that a line to an empty end produces more saccades, that is, scanning; that inference is this report's, not the authors'. No study has tested empty vanishing points in moving images or eye-tracked a film corridor.

Takeaway: The makers explain lines mechanically: a set built wall-to-wall so a lens can run its length, a 15mm chosen to keep stair lines clean, a dolly that makes a house grow. The menace of the empty vanishing point is the critics' reading, supported by one intact model paper and one still-image study, and by consistent craft testimony that the eye should be drawn down a line to a place that stays unresolved.

Balance: the weighted frame and the canted camera

The theory and its weak evidence

The film-school statement that an unbalanced frame creates unease descends from Arnheim and acquires no evidence on the way. Hübner and Fillinger note that Arnheim himself conceded most of his weight factors remained to be verified, and that McManus and colleagues, computing a physical centre of mass from pixel grey levels, concluded Arnheim's terms "cannot be taken literally" [6]. The craft restatements are consistent: StudioBinder says a frame where one side clearly outweighs the other "feels tense or tilted" [81], and Otto Ostera's summary of Arnheim gives the two factors that matter for horror, "An isolated object seems heavier than the same object accompanied by smaller ones all around it" and "the weight of an element increases in relation to its distance from the center" [82]. A lone figure pushed to one edge is heavy on both counts, and the empty side reads as a deficit the eye tries to fill.

Weighted to one side: the figure at the edge against emptiness

This is the balance device richest in makers' words, and it is the lead-room frame of section 2 in another vocabulary. Arochi's "blank space" where "fear lives behind your back" [17] and Cundey's anamorphic "lurking space" that is "psychologically stressful" [18] both weight the frame so that the figure and the light sit in one region and the opposite region is left dark or blank. Freddie Francis did the same in The Innocents (1961, director Jack Clayton): Wheeler Winston Dixon's critics' account is that Francis "responded to 20th Century Fox's insistence on CinemaScope by composing shots that are at once claustrophobic and yet sinuously inviting," darkening the edges so that "something is always lurking at the edges of the frame" [83]; sources differ on whether he painted the lens or used graded filters, and no Francis or Clayton statement has been found. Three decades of DPs (Francis 1961, Cundey 1978, Arochi 2024) independently cite Scope or large-format width as the enabling condition, which marks the technique as aspect-ratio dependent.

The canted and inverted frame

The canonical reference for the Dutch tilt is not horror: Wikipedia records, from Charles Thomas Samuels' interview with Carol Reed, that William Wyler gave Reed a spirit level after seeing The Third Man (1949) "to sardonically encourage him to use more traditional shooting angles" [84]. The Expressionist roots are in the sets rather than the camera, and no source in the record says Caligari uses a canted camera. Sam Raimi's Evil Dead tilts are documented by critics only: Wikipedia states that "In his Evil Dead trilogy, Sam Raimi used Dutch angles to show that a character had become possessed by evil" [84], and Cultural Gutter traces the tilt to Expressionism, "used to externalize a character's unbalanced state of mind" [85]; the production account in the makers' words concerns the shaky-cam rig that gave "the perspective of a demon," not the tilt [86].

Only two horror makers have been found explaining a canted or inverted frame in their own words. Sarroff on Smile's inverted skyline: Finn "definitely wanted the first one, where Rose is driving and the camera is parallel with her car"; for the skyline "we told the drone op to go freestyle, get weird"; and the rationale was "The world turned upside down to reflect Rose's state of mind" [20]. The interviewer counts three flips in the film and other inverted shots were cut, so "used sparingly" is borne out [20]. Pogorzelski explains how, not why, Midsommar's drive to Hårga rolls 180 degrees: the drone operators "at first they told us it was impossible to do that shot," and "The first day we flew it, the drone died as it took off" [87]. The why is critics' reading: F This Movie describes "our gods'-eye view of the car carrying the five main protagonists as it slowly begins to invert" as a message "that we've left the world we know for a much different reality" [88]. The two meanings, the protagonist's mind and a threshold into another world, are thus split between a maker (Sarroff) and critics (Midsommar); no Aster or Finn statement of intent has been found.

Awkward, fixed and "amateur" framing

In the found-footage and fixed-camera mode the imbalance is motivated diegetically, which is why makers explain it as credibility rather than composition. Oren Peli on Paranormal Activity (2007): "The credibility of the way we were presenting it was essential," and the locked frame let him "choose deliberately to do things off-camera" because the audience knows no cameraman is present; his favourite audience reaction is the groan of anticipation as the night shots fade in, before anything happens [89]. Kurosawa comes closest to a theory of the off-balance wide: he looks on location for "impossible angles and possibly unnatural lighting" and describes his method as "taking the world that an individual takes for granted, and then flipping it" [90]; Brian Eggert's critics' account of Cure (1997) is "master shots extended for long periods," against "the abrupt cutting and unstable framing of J-Horror" [91]. Skinamarink's corner-and-ceiling framing is documented only by critics (section 9).

Measured: balance preference is real, weak and disputed; discomfort is unmeasured

The balance literature is the weakest of the five. Wilson and Chatterjee's Assessment of Preference for Balance tracked liking of simple dot patterns well in Hübner and Fillinger's replication (R² = 0.752), but "Silvia and Barona (2009) could not replicate this result" [6]. McManus's fulcrum experiments found "the adjusted position of the fulcrum varied considerably, suggesting that art work is not generally well balanced," and Locher found that when paintings were disrupted "only the experts judged this version as less balanced" [6]. What is robust is Palmer's centre bias: "The most preferred position for every object in every facing direction was the frame's center," and "Off-center placements were not favored" [92]. Horror's off-centre, edge-crowded, outward-facing figure violates all three measured preferences at once. But no peer-reviewed study measuring arousal, skin conductance or eye movements in response to canted or unbalanced film frames has been found; "unbalanced equals unease" remains craft and textbook, and Locher's expert split suggests subtle imbalance registers consciously only for trained viewers.

Takeaway: The weighted frame is the best-documented balance device, with three decades of DPs describing the same move in Scope or large format. The canted frame is rarer and its only horror explanation on record is Sarroff's; Midsommar's roll is explained only as engineering. The preference for centred, balanced frames is measured and modest; the discomfort of its violation is not measured at all.

08

Measured versus lore: the preferences are proven, the dread is not

As of Oct 23, 2026 the empirical record divides into three tiers, and none of the five devices has a study in the top tier for the feeling horror claims from it.

The first tier is measured defaults in viewing, which the rules codify. Centre bias and the inward bias are replicated in the laboratory, in free photography and in centuries of animal art [7], [24], [92]. Sharpness attracts first fixations and blur is ignored rather than repelled [11], [60]. Conspicuity defined by contrast with the surround attracts gaze [10], and in moving images motion and flicker attract it more strongly than static contrast [9]. Mainstream editing produces overwhelming attentional synchrony [12]. Converging lines improve fixation prediction when added to saliency models [79], and a subject at the end of a line reduces saccades and lengthens fixation in still photographs [80]. Balance preference exists but is weak, stimulus dependent and disputed [6].

The second tier is what those defaults imply for horror's inversions, and it is inference. If viewers prefer centred, inward-facing, balanced subjects, then a short-sided, edge-crowded figure is measurably less pleasing, which is consistent with "unnerving" and "wrong-footed" but does not show fear. If blur is ignored, a soft ghost is not hidden from attention so much as outcompeted, which is consistent with Nakata and Duscio but has not been tested on a moving rack or on horror footage. If a line to an empty end produces more saccades, that scanning is what makers call lurking space, but the study that suggests it had no empty-line control and did not measure affect. If motion beats contrast, a dark frame that stays still is the regime in which Lewton's prepared darkness is the dominant cue, but no study isolates contrast in dark frames.

The third tier is lore, meaning claims stated consistently by makers and critics and measured by nobody: short-siding as isolation and wrongness (Campbell), the empty side as the place where fear lives (Arochi), the silhouette as threat category without identity (Cundey, Blaschke), the soft ghost as shape before identity (Nakata), the empty vanishing point as dread (critics of Kubrick), the canted frame as a mind or a world turned over (Sarroff, critics of Midsommar), and the trapped runner against the leading edge (teaching material only). So do the affective words themselves: dread, threat, isolation, a world gone wrong. The lore is not weak because it is lore; Arochi, Cundey, Sarroff, Gioulakis, Duscio, Nakata and Mitchell describe the same mechanisms independently across decades, formats and countries, which is strong craft evidence. But no eye-tracking, skin-conductance or self-report study has tested any of the five inversions on horror stimuli, and no focus-puller or editor account of a horror scare has been found online. Where a sentence in this report says a device produces dread, that is the makers' claim, reported as such.

Caveats, disputed facts and gaps

Several sources could be read only on reposts or not at all as of Oct 23, 2026, and the citations follow what was actually read. Every Filmmaker Magazine interview used here (Arochi on Longlegs, Pogorzelski on Midsommar) was blocked at the original URL and is cited to Deep Fried Movies, which reposts the interviews with attribution and is named in the text. British Cinematographer, Criterion, the Journal of Vision page for Smith and Mital, and the ASC's members-only Cundey videos could not be read; the Smith and Mital and Mital 2011 results are paraphrased from institutional records rather than quoted. The standard textbooks (Blain Brown, Mascelli, Katz, Mercado, Block) could not be quoted from a working URL; the rules are cited to Wikipedia's lead-room entry, a working DP's blog, an ASC column, StudioBinder and a peer-reviewed summary of Arnheim instead, and page-accurate textbook wording should be taken from print.

Several claims common in the literature were dropped because they reached the researchers only as search snippets or from unreliable hosts: Bordwell's line about rack focus "smoothly" guiding attention; Whannell's Bloody Disgusting remarks about filming as if the Invisible Man were visible; the figure that It Follows was shot roughly 80 percent on an 18mm; Arnheim's own sentences on obliqueness; and an AI-written glossary's claims about The Silence of the Lambs being short-sided and about short-siding signalling liars.

Four corrections to common lore are honoured throughout. Halloween's Laurie-and-Michael doorway reveal is a lighting effect, a blue source beside the mask, not a focus pull. Rosemary's Baby is a wide-lens, deep-field film shot on 18mm and 25mm lenses, and its leaning-in effect comes from occlusion at a door edge, not blur. Hereditary's ceiling shot hides Annie with darkness and upper-corner placement, not focus, and no maker statement about it exists. The Borji and Feng arXiv paper on vanishing points was withdrawn by its author; the intact result is the earlier WACV model paper, cited with that caveat.

Disputed or weakly attributed facts include whether Freddie Francis darkened The Innocents' edges with paint on the lens or graded glass filters (sources differ); Aster's reported Midsommar line that daylight "forces you to confront everything head-on" (quoted without a named publication, so omitted); and Wilson and Chatterjee's exact abstract wording (their PDF would not text-extract, so their figures come from Hübner and Fillinger's replication).

The maker-statement gaps are large and worth naming for anyone extending this work: no horror DP or director has been found using the term "short-siding" about their own film; no Carpenter or Cundey quote describes Halloween's street and hedge compositions; no Shimizu, Hayashi, Musuraca, Boulton or Vanlint statement on lighting or focus has been found online; no Raimi explanation of the Evil Dead tilts, no Hitchcock statement on Psycho's Arbogast angle, no Kubrick statement on vanishing points, no Finn or Aster statement on the inversions, and no Ball statement on Skinamarink's corner framing; and no focus puller or editor has been found describing a horror scare. The Blu-ray commentaries and the members-only ASC videos are the likely primary sources for most of these.
