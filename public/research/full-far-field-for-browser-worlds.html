A full far field: how games fill the distance

How shipped open-world games keep the distance looking full, from far terrain and vegetation to the camera-side tricks that hide the seams, for a browser frame.

  • 15 min read

Part 2 of 2 in Filling the distance · Previous: A full far ground without drawing it

Question: A world that reads as photographic at arm's length tends to fall apart past a few dozen metres. The grass stops at a line and a flat plane carries on behind it. The ground goes to one tiling texture, then to one colour. The trees become dark cut-outs with ragged edges against the sky. Hills meet forest along a hard seam. The whole far field reads as sparse and low in polygons, and the eye, which trusts the distance to say how big a place is, stops believing the place. How do shipped open-world games keep the far field looking full, and what do they do on the camera's side, with fog, scattering, blur and anti-aliasing, to hide what they could not afford to draw? And which of those answers fit a browser game on WebGL2 or WebGPU, with a 16.7 ms frame and a download budget?

Short answer: the distance looks thin for one reason with three faces. Every system that makes the near field rich (grass blades, detail textures, full tree meshes, shadow maps) stops at a radius, and past that radius nothing stands in for it: the ground loses the colour and the roughness the cover gave it, the vegetation loses its mass, and the silhouettes lose their softness. Shipped games answer each face in the same way: they keep a cheaper stand-in running all the way to the horizon, and they colour-match the stand-in to the thing it replaces so the handover is invisible. Terrain carries a baked far albedo and normal, a macro colour map and a detail layer that fades out by distance, cached in a virtual texture [1], [2]. Vegetation runs as a chain of levels that ends in billboards or impostors, and grass thins and widens by rule until it becomes a texture on the ground [3], [4]. The handovers are dithered under temporal anti-aliasing, the alpha edges come from signed distance fields so they stay clean when small, and the far shadows come from a coarse cascade with a cross-fade [3], [5]. Then the camera does the rest: aerial perspective desaturates and lifts the distance toward the sky by depth [6], fog hides what the budget could not draw [7], specular is filtered so far surfaces stop sparkling [8], [9], and temporal anti-aliasing turns sub-pixel foliage into a soft mass instead of noise. For a browser, the cheap wins are the colour-matched far ground, a density ramp for the cover with a far-ground texture under it, an impostor atlas for far trees, per-pixel aerial perspective from the sky model, and dithered fades under temporal anti-aliasing; the costly ones are virtual texturing and full impostor parallax.

01

Why the distance looks thin

Near-field richness comes from systems with a radius: geometry grass and ground cover out to a few tens of metres, tiling detail textures that only make sense while a texel is bigger than a pixel, full tree meshes out to some tens or hundreds of metres, cascaded shadows out to a cascade's far plane. Each radius is a budget decision, and each radius is also a seam. At the seam the eye sees three things at once:

  • The cover stops. Blades and cards end at a line, and the ground behind the line is a plane with a texture on it. If the plane's colour is the soil's and not the cover's, the line is a change of colour as well as of relief, and nothing hides it.
  • The surface goes flat. A detail texture tiled at a metre becomes, at a hundred metres, a single averaged colour with a faint repeat in it, with none of the variation a real hillside has at that scale: drifts of colour, wet and dry, the shadow of a slope. The normal map averages to flat as well, so the far ground stops responding to light.
  • The silhouettes go to confetti. An alpha-tested crown that was a tree at twenty metres is, at two hundred, a cloud of pixels, each on or off. Without anti-aliasing on the alpha edge the crown reads as a dark cut-out with a ragged rim, lit as a flat card.

Every technique below attacks one of these three. The thread through all of them is continuity: a far stand-in that is tuned to look like the near thing's average, and a handover that nobody can point at.

02

Terrain at distance

2.1 Bake the far look and fade the detail into it

Far Cry 5's terrain is a quad tree of 64 m tiles, six levels deep, streamed by distance from the camera, and every node carries a baked payload: a height map, a world-space normal map that also packs smoothness and specular occlusion, an albedo map, a colour modulation map and a splat map [1]. The far terrain is shaded directly and cheaply from that baked albedo and normal, and only the near terrain pays for the splat map and its detail textures, which are sampled by world position, blended across the four nearest splat texels by their material heights, and cached into a virtual texture so the expensive blend runs once a page rather than once a pixel [1]. The team's stated goal was that rocks, trees and grass "blend with, and be grounded in, the terrain", at about 2 ms of GPU for the whole terrain full screen [1].

Battlefield 3's terrain took the same shape a generation earlier: a height field with the mesh built at runtime, shaded by procedural splatting (arbitrary shaders applied by painted masks) with a colour map used as an overlay on top of the splat, and the whole result cached into a procedural virtual texture at 32 samples a metre so the splat's cost does not scale with view distance, with a per-tile fade factor that blends a newly composited tile in over its predecessor [2]. The point of both designs is the same: the far field is a cached, baked picture of the near field's average, not a cheaper shader that looks different.

Engines without a virtual texture ship the same idea as a blend. Open 3D Engine's terrain has a macro material (a low-frequency colour and normal over the whole terrain) and a detail material blended over it near the camera, with a cutoff distance and a fade range past which only the macro remains [10]. The macro is what the eye sees from far away in any case, so the fade removes nothing it could have seen; what it adds is the large-scale variation the detail texture never had.

2.2 Keep the far surface alive

Three details make a far terrain read as real rather than as a plane:

  • A colour modulation map. Far Cry 5 carries one in every node beside the albedo [1]; Battlefield 3 paints a colour map over the splat [2]. It is the drift of colour across a hillside at the ten-to-hundred-metre scale: wetter, greener, more mineral, more dead. Without it, a far hillside has exactly one colour, and that is the single strongest tell of a game world from the distance.
  • Screen-constant tiling on steep ground. Cliffs are seen from far away, so a tiling scale tuned for the near field repeats visibly across a whole mountainside. Far Cry 5 changes the tiling scale with distance so the texture's period stays roughly constant in screen space, which works because rock textures are close to fractal and read at more than one scale [1].
  • Stochastic blends where blends are expensive. For far cliffs, Far Cry 5 picks one material and one projection per pixel by a stable hashed noise instead of blending them all, and alpha-blends only two tiling scales on top: eight texture samples instead of forty-eight, with the noise invisible because the far colour data has so little contrast [1]. The lesson generalises: at a distance, noise that averages right is cheaper than arithmetic that is right, and the eye cannot tell them apart.

2.3 Ground what stands on the terrain

Objects that sit on the ground give the far field away when their footprint is a hard edge. Far Cry 5 renders rock and root decals into the terrain's virtual texture and gives each a small matching mesh for shape, so the object's colour is already part of the ground before its geometry stands on it [1]. The principle for any engine: whatever cover a hillside has, its ground colour under and around the cover should already be the cover's average, so that when the cover's geometry stops the colour does not.

03

Vegetation at distance

3.1 A chain that ends in a card

Horizon Zero Dawn's trees run five levels: about 10,000 triangles, 2,600, 1,200, then about 200 triangles with a 12-triangle billboard fading in, then the billboard alone; plants run four, ending in an 8-triangle billboard; grass runs three, from 20 to 36 triangles down to 10 to 18 on a cheaper shader [3]. Two decisions in that chain do the far field's work. The billboard does not replace the last mesh at a line; it fades in alongside it over the fourth level, so the crown's mass never drops. And the vegetation is colourised in the shader from world data (erosion, flow, closeness to water, baked into a 512 by 512 world texture) and by ecotope, with an "illuminate" blend that tints the albedo [3], which means the near mesh and the far card draw their colour from the same source and cannot drift apart. Far Cry 4 moved the same way, from modelled leaves up close to tessellated, displaced billboards at a distance that still carry the wind [11].

The modern card is the octahedral impostor: a single sprite of 8 triangles whose texture holds a grid of captures of the mesh from directions laid out over a hemisphere, with the three captured frames nearest the current view blended at runtime. Epic's baker stores base colour and normal, with optional depth, roughness and subsurface, at 2048 pixels for its own projects; Fortnite uses a 12 by 12 grid, 144 frames, for most of its distant foliage, and upper-hemisphere impostors for all its trees when Nanite is off, because they look better from far away than billboards [12]. Lighting works because the impostor carries a normal map and the engine lights it as it lights the mesh; a parallax mode traces the depth captures so the card has thickness near the handover [12]. On the far field the impostor's value is not its cheapness so much as its stability: a tree that keeps the same colour, normal response and silhouette mass from 50 m to 500 m, under the same sun.

3.2 Grass: thin by rule, then become the ground

Ghost of Tsushima draws its fields as individual blades generated on the GPU, and its distance scheme is the clearest statement of the rule [4]. Near blades have 15 vertices, far blades 7 with a simpler curve, and the near shape blends toward the far shape as it nears the switch so there is no pop. Tiles double in size with distance and keep the same blade count, so far grass stands twice as far apart; the near grass drops three blades in four as it approaches that line, gradually, so the density already matches when the tile size changes. Beyond the last tile the field is replaced by a texture on the terrain, and blades past the maximum distance are culled on the GPU before a vertex is built [4], [13]. Horizon's grass scales its whole animation down with distance and pushes its vertices toward the ground by a percentage of the object's height, so far grass is shorter, calmer and denser-looking per pixel than near grass [3].

A single-plane far grass is a legitimate stand-in when it is shaded like the grass it replaces. One Godot write-up builds its far plane from the same world-space noise the blades use for their patches, a baked top-down normal map of real blades, matched backlight, roughness and specular constants, and a view-angle term that shows more ground through the plane when the camera looks down and more blade tips when it looks across; the impostor fades in from 5 to 10 units and the blades fade out from 10 to 20, by dithered alpha, and the whole thing costs under 2 ms [14]. The numbers are one project's; the structure is the industry's: the far plane is not a flat colour but a shaded surface built from the near grass's own ingredients.

3.3 Density by distance, not by cutoff

Battlefield 3 stores terrain decoration (trees, bushes, grass) as instance lists in quad tree payloads at several depths, and overlaps them: a payload near the root holds a sparse set of trees seen from far away, the next level adds more trees at a medium distance, the next adds bushes near the camera, each level only adding to what the coarser one already drew [2]. The far field therefore always holds the trees that matter at that distance, and density grows toward the camera without a line where it begins.

04

The handover

4.1 Dither the fade and let the anti-aliasing resolve it

A cross-fade between two levels of detail by alpha blending costs sorting and overdraw; the common alternative is a dithered opacity mask whose threshold ramps over the transition while both levels draw, so each pixel shows one level or the other, and temporal anti-aliasing, whose noise pattern changes every frame, averages the two into a smooth blend over a few frames [5]. The pattern is visible without temporal history, and both levels are drawn for a fraction of a second, which are the costs. Far Cry 5's stochastic material blends rest on the same insight, stabilised with the position hash and screen gradients of hashed alpha testing so the noise neither swims under camera motion nor aliases [1], [15].

4.2 Keep the alpha edge clean when it is small

A crown's alpha channel, mipmapped, turns into a soft blur that alpha-testing then cuts at an arbitrary level, which is where confetti comes from. Horizon stores its vegetation alpha as a signed distance texture, so the mip chain stays a clean edge at every size and the artists control the quality over distance by hand [3]. Signed distance alpha is cheap to produce offline and free at runtime, and it fixes the ragged far silhouette at its source.

4.3 Far shadows from a coarse cascade

Horizon's sun shadows run four cascades: a 1024 map for the player alone, a 2048 map to 8 m, a 2048 map to 80 m, and a distant 1536 map covering a 1.5 km square from a height-field based system, with a smooth alpha-blended cross-fade between cascades; vegetation casts shadows from separate, cheaper meshes, alpha-tested and animated in the near cascades and neither in the far one [3]. The far field then keeps its slope shadows and tree-line shadows, which are most of what makes a hillside read as three-dimensional, without paying for a full-resolution map over kilometres.

05

The camera's part

5.1 Aerial perspective is the far field's main light

The single strongest cue of distance in a landscape is the air itself: light scattered into the view path lifts far surfaces toward the sky's colour and lowers their contrast, and light scattered out of it attenuates what they reflect. Hillaire's atmosphere model computes exactly that per pixel with a small set of look-up tables: a transmittance table, a sky-view table for the distant sky, and a camera-frustum volume whose texels hold the in-scattered luminance and the transmittance from the camera to that depth, applied to every scene pixel by its depth [6]. The result is the hazing and the blueing of the far hillside, decoupled from screen resolution and costing about a millisecond for the tables. For the far field this does three jobs at once: it hides the loss of detail by lowering the contrast where detail is gone, it unifies the ground, the trees and the sky into one palette by depth, and it tells the eye how far away the far field is, which is the thing a thin far field fails to do.

5.2 Fog has always hidden the budget

Distance fog was used, in Wronski's words, as very thick linear fog to hide a very short streaming distance, and volumetric fog is the modern, physically-shaped version of the same instrument [7]. A height fog that thickens in valleys and a distance term that follows the weather are not only atmosphere: they are the knob that sets where the far field's substitutes must still convince and where the air may take over. Real haze falls off exponentially, not linearly, and a fog that reaches its colour too early reads as a wall.

5.3 Stop the sparkle

Far surfaces shine wrongly because mipmapping averages a normal map's direction but not its roughness, so a rough surface seen from afar keeps the gloss of its bumps and sparkles. LEAN mapping stores the bump statistics in a form that filters linearly, so the highlight widens as the bumps shrink below a pixel [8]; the cheaper Toksvig correction reads the averaged normal's shortened length as a roughness increase and needs nothing but the normal map already in hand [9]. On a far field, a roughness ramp with distance in the shader does most of the same work: whatever the detail normal said, the far hillside and the far water should go matte.

5.4 Resolution, mip bias and temporal filtering

Temporal anti-aliasing matters more at a distance than anywhere else: it is what turns an alpha-tested crown's sub-pixel leaves into a soft mass, a dithered fade into a blend, and a stochastic material blend into a colour [5], [15]. Without it the far field is noise. A negative mip bias on foliage alpha and ground detail sharpens the mid field at the price of shimmer that temporal filtering then has to absorb; a positive bias on the far field softens it on purpose. Depth of field is the last veil, used sparingly at distance: a slight far blur reads as a long lens and hides the far field's edges, but it also removes the sharpness that says "far", so it is a lens choice rather than a fix.

06

What fits a browser frame

A browser build has the same three faces of the problem and a tighter budget, so the order is by payoff per millisecond.

  1. Colour-match the far ground to the cover. The ground under the cover should be the cover's average colour and roughness, read from the same source, so the cover's edge is a change of relief and not of colour. This is a shader constant and a texture choice, and it removes the sharpest line in the frame. A far-ground texture built from a top-down render of the near cover, tiled with world-space noise, carries it further for one texture sample.
  2. Thin the cover by rule and let the ground take over. Drop density toward the edge in steps the eye cannot see, widen what remains so coverage holds, and fade the last of it by dithered alpha under the temporal pass. The edge then has no line to point at.
  3. Give far trees an impostor atlas with normals. One sprite a tree, a hemisphere of captures, three frames blended, lit by the same sun as the near mesh, and a crown alpha from a signed distance field. On WebGL2 the capture is an offline bake; the runtime is three texture reads.
  4. Add a macro colour map to the terrain. A single low-frequency colour and roughness variation over the world, blended in by distance, so a far hillside is a field of drifts and not a colour. It costs one texture read and it is the difference between a plane and a landscape.
  5. Compute aerial perspective per pixel from the sky model. A scene that already has a sky model has everything the froxel volume needs; even the analytic single-scatter version, applied by depth, unifies the far field's palette and contrast.
  6. Ramp roughness with distance and keep a far shadow cascade. Both are a few lines of shader and a cascade setting, and both stop the far field from looking like plastic under a flat light.

What does not fit: a virtual texture cache, which needs a page table, a feedback pass and a compositing pipeline; full impostor parallax at every tree; and shadow maps over kilometres at near-field resolution. The far field in a browser is a well-matched set of stand-ins under a believable atmosphere, which is, in the end, what the far field is in every shipped game too.
