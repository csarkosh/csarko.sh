Scene depth for a post process: WebGPU against WebGL2

  • 16 min read
  • Also on GitHub

Question: A screen-space effect that needs to know how far away each pixel is, a depth-aware blur, a fog, an outline, an ambient occlusion, has to get the scene's depth into a texture the post process can read. On WebGL2 that has been done one of two or three ways for a decade. A renderer that runs on WebGPU as well, which in a browser engine such as Babylon.js is one flag away, finds that the same job meets the two APIs' differences one after another. Which of them bite, which are the engine's rather than the API's, and what does a lost GPU look like on each?

Short answer: the depth itself comes from the same three places on both APIs: a second pass over the scene with a depth-only shader, the scene's own depth buffer, or the scene drawn once into several targets with depth as one of them. The third is the right one for a game that is bound by its draw count, and it is what we shipped. On the way the two APIs parted in six places. WGSL's uniformity analysis refuses a shader that samples a texture after a branch on a per-pixel value, which GLSL compiles without a word, and the fix is an explicit sample level [1]. A draw that leaves one of several targets untouched is a framebuffer state on WebGL2 (drawBuffers) and a pipeline's colour write mask on WebGPU, so one call in the engine becomes two different objects [2], [3]. Multisampling several targets is a blit per attachment on one and a resolve target per attachment on the other [4], [5]. The depth's own varying counts against a device limit WebGPU is asked for up front, which one material already filled [6]. A 32-bit float texture is filterable on WebGL2 only with an extension and on WebGPU only with a device feature, and WebGPU makes the mismatch a validation error rather than a wrong result [7], [8]. And losing the GPU is a lost context on WebGL2 but a lost device on WebGPU, which when the browser's GPU process goes takes WebGL2 down with it [9], [10]. Three more traps were the engine's and the same on both: a prepass that reads the wrong camera's post-process chain and switches itself off, one that rewrites the scene's image-processing flag every frame, and one that takes up every render target the scene draws, impostor bakes among them.

The worked example is Day Hike, a first-person hiking game in Babylon.js 9.18, public at game-dayhike and playable at games.csarko.sh/dayhike. Its high tier draws with WebGPU in Chrome and Edge and everything else draws with WebGL2 (WebGPU and WebGL2: what changes and what does not). The post process in question softens the far field, part of the work in A full far field: past 80 m of camera depth the frame is blurred by a radius rising to 5 texels at 450 m, each read kept only where its own depth is not much nearer than the pixel's, so near grass against the far field keeps its edge. The machine throughout is an Apple M4 running Chrome 157, WebGPU through Metal and WebGL2 through ANGLE's Metal backend.

01

Three places a depth can come from

Every renderer that wants a depth texture for a post process has the same three options, and the choice is the same on both APIs.

A second pass with a depth-only shader. Babylon's DepthRenderer draws the scene again into a target of its own, with a shader that writes vViewPos.z and nothing else [11]. It needs nothing of the materials and works everywhere, and that is also its flaw: it draws every mesh again, and with its own vertex stage, so anything a material plugin did to the geometry (cards turned to face the camera, grass bent by wind, far cover widened with distance) is missing from its depth. A scene that draws several hundred thin-instanced buckets a frame pays for all of them twice.

The scene's own depth buffer. If the scene is multisampled, which an alpha-tested world wants, the depth attachment cannot be read: WebGL2 cannot blit a multisampled depth renderbuffer into a texture a sampler reads [4], and WebGPU allows no resolve target on a depth attachment [5]. A single-sampled scene gives the depth away for free and the edges with it.

The scene drawn once into several targets. Babylon's PrePassRenderer draws the scene into a multiple render target, the materials' own shaders writing colour to the first attachment and whatever was asked for, here camera-space depth, to the others [12]. Every vertex plugin and every alpha test holds in the depth, nothing is drawn twice, and both APIs take the same path. A post process asks for the depth by declaring it in a configuration the prepass reads when it updates, the prepass's targets become the first post process's input, and the multisampling moves to them.

Day Hike built the first and shipped the third. The rest of this note is what the third asked of each API, and what the first revealed on the way.

02

A branch the GLSL accepted and the WGSL refused

The blur leaves early where its radius is under a fifth of a texel, which is most of the frame, and samples its rings only past that:

float r = smoothstep(soft.x, soft.y, d) * soft.z;
if (r < 0.2) { gl_FragColor = c; return; }
for (int i = 0; i < 12; i++) { ... texture2D(textureSampler, vUV + o) ... }

WebGL2 compiled and drew it. WebGPU refused the shader:

error: 'textureSample' must only be called from uniform control flow
note: control flow depends on possibly non-uniform value
  if ((x_56 < 0.20000000298023223877f)) {
note: return value of 'textureSample' may be non-uniform
  let x_24 : vec4<f32> = textureSample(depthSamplerTexture, depthSamplerSampler, x_22);

This is WGSL's uniformity analysis [1]. An implicit-level sample needs screen-space derivatives, derivatives need every invocation in a quad to reach the same call, and a branch on a value read from a texture cannot promise that; so the compiler rejects the program rather than leave the result undefined. GLSL ES 3.00 has the same hazard and makes it the author's problem: a derivative taken inside non-uniform control flow is undefined, the driver returns something, and nothing is reported [13]. The same text is a silent undefined value on one API and a compile error on the other.

The fix is the same on both. Every read goes to an explicit level, textureLod(sampler, uv, 0.0), which the translator turns into textureSampleLevel, a builtin the analysis leaves alone because it takes no derivative [1]. The early leave stays. The lesson generalises: on WebGPU, sample at an explicit level in any post process that branches on what it reads, which is every depth-aware one.

Where the error surfaces depends on where the translation happens. Babylon turns GLSL into WGSL through SPIR-V and Tint, at run time or ahead of it into a map the build ships [14], so a shader that fails does so the first time that stage is translated: on a machine without the shipped map, the first time the page opens. What the failure did to the page is the subject of section 6.

03

A draw that leaves a target alone

The prepass compiles every capable material with as many outputs as there are targets. A material that cannot write them all, in Babylon any ShaderMaterial, is drawn with only the first target enabled, and the renderer tells the engine so through one call, bindAttachments, that the two engines answer differently:

  • WebGL2 calls gl.drawBuffers([COLOR_ATTACHMENT0, NONE]) [2]. The framebuffer keeps its attachments; the draw writes the first and leaves the second as it was. It is a state of the framebuffer, set between draws, and costs nothing.
  • WebGPU has no such call. A render pass's colour attachments are fixed when the pass begins, and a fragment stage must declare an output for every target it writes [3]. Babylon's engine keeps the attachment list on its pipeline cache and bakes it into the render pipeline as a colour write mask of zero for the targets the draw leaves alone [15]. The target stays bound, the shader writes nothing to it, and the pipeline is a different pipeline from the one that writes both, so a scene with such materials carries two pipelines per material state instead of one.

Babylon's PBR shader carries the seam in its last lines:

#ifdef PREPASS
#include<pbrBlockPrePass>
#endif
#if !defined(PREPASS) || defined(WEBGL2)
gl_FragColor=finalColor;
#endif

On WebGL2 gl_FragColor and gl_FragData[0] are the same output and both lines run; on WebGPU only the prepass block writes [16]. Anything a material plugin does to the final colour after the prepass block lands on one API and not the other. Day Hike's plugins all write before it, at the hook above the block, so the two engines draw the same colour; it is the kind of line to know about before the first "WebGPU looks different" report.

What the untouched target holds is the clear value, which is 0 on both. The blur reads 0 as the far sky, so a far tree against the sky blurs with it as it should, and nothing the sky dome does not write needs a special case.

The depth itself costs a varying. Every capable material's vertex stage gains a vViewPos output for the prepass to write the depth from, and on WebGPU that output counts against maxInterStageShaderVariables, a device limit a renderer asks for up front [6]. Day Hike's giant trees' faded material already filled the 21 it asked for on the high tier (three shadow cascades, a two-sided fragment reading front_facing), and its pipeline failed with Total fragment input variables count (22 = 21 (user-defined) + 1 (front_facing) exceeds the maximum (21), an uncaptured error that rebuilt the renderer on WebGL2 once more. The limit the device is asked for rose to 22; the Apple M4 adapter offers 28. WebGL2 has the same ceiling in MAX_VARYING_VECTORS, but counts vectors the compiler packs scalars into and raised no error here [17].

04

Multisampling several targets at once

The scene is drawn at 4 samples a pixel. With one target that is a multisampled renderbuffer resolved into a texture on WebGL2, or a multisampled texture with a resolveTarget on WebGPU, and the post process reads the resolved texture. With two targets each needs its own:

  • WebGL2 attaches a multisampled renderbuffer per colour attachment and, at the end of the pass, blits each into its texture with blitFramebuffer, one read and one draw binding per attachment [4].
  • WebGPU gives each colour attachment of the render pass a multisampled texture and a resolveTarget, and the resolve is part of ending the pass [5].

Babylon hides both behind one setter on the multiple render target [18]. The engine sets that instead of the first post process's own sample count, which with the prepass in front of it would multisample a target nothing draws into. The depth target resolves along with the colour, which averages the depth across a pixel's samples at an edge; for a gate that asks only whether a neighbour is much nearer, that is fine, and it is the same on both APIs.

05

Which float a sampler may filter

The prepass stores its depth in a 32-bit float texture where the engine can filter one, otherwise in a 16-bit float. Both APIs render to both; what they let a sampler do with them differs:

  • WebGL2 filters a 32-bit float texture only with OES_texture_float_linear; without it a linear sampler on one returns an incomplete texture's value, and no error [7].
  • WebGPU makes filtering a validation matter. A 32-bit float texture has the sample type unfilterable-float unless the device was requested with the float32-filterable feature, and binding it to a filtering sampler is a pipeline error [8]. Babylon's WebGPU engine checks the texture's type against the device when it builds the bind group layout and, where the texture cannot be filtered, demotes the sampler to non-filtering and the binding to unfilterable-float on its own [15].

The blur reads the depth at the texel's centre at an explicit level, so it never needs filtering and the demotion costs it nothing. A pass that did bilinear reads of a 32-bit depth would run the same shader text on both APIs and get filtered depth on one and point-sampled depth on the other.

06

Losing the GPU

Three of the first nine runs on WebGPU lost the device, about 25 s after the page opened, and each time the renderer the engine rebuilt lost it again some 20 s later; after the second loss the page could not make a WebGL2 context either, since the browser's GPU process had gone with the device. Chrome's own log carried one line before each loss, A valid external Instance reference no longer exists, and nothing from Dawn's validation layer [19]. The losses came with a depth texture bound and read by the post process, twice from the depth renderer's target and once from the prepass's; a pass that bound the depth and read a constant instead never lost it, nor did the depth pass drawing with no post process reading it, nor any of six later runs that read the depth, with and without Dawn's backend validation. The cause is not found. The correlation with reading a float render target in a post process is the strongest there is, and the runs that survived say it is not that alone.

What a lost GPU looks like is itself an API difference:

  • WebGL2 raises webglcontextlost on the canvas, and webglcontextrestored when the browser has a context again; the application is expected to handle both and rebuild its resources [9]. The GPU process is usually still there, and a new context on a new canvas works.
  • WebGPU resolves the device's lost promise with a reason and a message, the device and everything made from it stop working, and the application requests a new adapter and device [10]. When the GPU process itself went down, every API it hosted goes with it, WebGL2 included, until the browser restarts it.

Day Hike's failure path rebuilds the renderer on a fresh canvas, once on WebGPU again and then on WebGL2, pins the tab to WebGL2 and remembers the fall-back for the browser version; on the runs above the second rebuild found no GPU to build on at any tier, and the page kept its world with no renderer. The player sees the pause screen, because a rebuild releases the pointer, which is how the problem was first noticed: a still of the trailhead came out with the pause menu over it, and the shader error of section 2, reported by Babylon as an uncaptured GPU error, had rebuilt the renderer on WebGL2 underneath it. A shader that fails on one API is not a visual bug there; it is an engine change.

A renderer cannot ship a one-in-three device loss in a feature. The softening shipped on both engines because every run after the first nine, among them a full set of seven stills at 75 s each, drew with the prepass and lost nothing, and a lost device is an event the game's telemetry carries with its reason. If it returns, the telemetry will say on which engine.

07

Three traps that were the same on both

Not every difference met was the API's. Three were the engine's, the same on WebGPU and WebGL2, and they took longer to find than the API differences did, because a prepass that is silently off draws the same frame on both engines, with no depth in it.

  • The prepass reads the chain from the scene's active camera. It decides what it draws for by walking the active camera's post processes when it updates, which is at the first draw after anything marks it dirty: a pass attached or detached, its own creation [12]. The first draw of a frame is as often a render target's, a mirror or an impostor bake, whose own camera is the active one while it draws and has no chain. The prepass then finds nothing, switches itself off, and does not look again until something marks it dirty. The fix is to run its update with the chain's camera active.
  • The prepass writes the image-processing flag every frame. It sets the scene's image processing to run in a post process only when it finds the engine's own image-processing pass in the chain [12]. A renderer with a grade pass of its own gets the flag set false, every lit material tone-maps in its shader, and the grade pass tone-maps the frame again. The fix is to pin the flag.
  • The prepass takes up every render target the scene draws. The first time a render target clears, the prepass gives it a prepass target of its own and updates, which re-reads every material's defines [12]. A renderer that bakes impostors into render targets during play gets a re-read with each bake, the bake catches its materials mid-recompile and keeps nothing, and the far trees stand as bare poles, on both APIs alike. The fix is to opt every render target of one's own out of the prepass (noPrePassRenderer), so it draws for the scene's camera alone.

08

What a new shader variant costs to ship

Turning the prepass on compiles every capable material with a new define. On WebGL2 that is a recompile the browser does the first time it draws each material, from the GLSL the page already has. On WebGPU each stage must be translated to WGSL, and a renderer that wants a first visit never to wait on the translator ships the translations: Day Hike builds a map of them from a recorded corpus, one per quality tier [14], and a new define on every material is a new stage for every material, so the corpus is recorded again before the map is built. Where the map is missing, the page translates at run time and its render pipelines land one by one, so a still of the meadow shot at 55 s on WebGPU showed the trees as bare poles with their crowns' pipelines still to come, and the same still at 110 s showed them whole; on WebGL2 the same page at 55 s was complete. The difference is not the engine's speed. It is where the translation happens, and WebGPU moves it to the author.

09

What was not measured

No frame times were taken for the prepass against the depth renderer or against no softening, on either API; the prepass was chosen on what it does not do, a second pass, and on the correctness of its depth, not on a measurement. The device loss has no reproduction. Both belong in the next measurement: the cost of the second target and its resolve at 4 samples on each API, and a run long enough to say whether the loss returns.
