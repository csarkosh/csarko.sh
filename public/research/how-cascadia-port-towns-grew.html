How Cascadia's coastal port towns came to look the way they do

Sawmills, railroads and the 1889 to 1893 speculation placed Washington's coast port towns; 21 street grids measured from OpenStreetMap show how each differs.

  • 24 min read

Question: Why do the port towns of coastal Washington, from Puget Sound to the mouth of the Columbia, stand where they do and look as they do? And what does that say about building one for a game?

Short answer: Sawmills, governments and railroads put the towns at sheltered deep water and the heads of navigation. There was often no flat ground, so they went up on pilings and then fill. All were laid out as grids; each differs by where its grids meet, where the land broke them and what burned. Of 21 towns measured, 9 have two or three grids. A generator should decide a town's history first and derive its form from it.

What that leaves open: The history comes from published essays, mostly HistoryLink's, and the measurements from one OpenStreetMap download with limits: street centre lines, hand-chosen boxes, uneven tagging. Neither says what a generated town should be, and the Native nations' own accounts are the right source for their places, so only what their own pages and the treaties say is used here. By the end you can see which numbers are this doc's own and check every claim against its source.

The figure is the doc's own measurement: look at how many dots each town has, and how far the second dot sits from the first, which is the angle at which two grids meet.

In numbers:

  • 4,798 feet: the wharf Olympia built in 1885 to reach deep water, needed until the Corps dredged in 1895 (section 1).
  • 150 to 3,500, then $2.5 million to $414,320: South Bend's population from 1889 to 1894, then its tax valuation from 1891 to 1895 (section 2).
  • 12, 7 and 2: towns with one, two and three street grids, of 21 measured (section 4).
  • 335 by 365 feet is Aberdeen's median block; Port Angeles's is 372 by 560 (section 4).

01

Shelter, deep water, timber and the head of navigation chose the sites, and the sites often had no flat ground

In 1907 a journalist described Aberdeen, Washington, this way: "Many of her streets are of sawdust, while all the principal streets are of plank; piling is driven as a foundation for her cement, brick or stone business blocks; many of her residences stand on piles and at high tide, water is seen through the cracks in the boardwalks as well as surrounding the houses" [1]. Aberdeen sits where the Chehalis and Wishkah rivers meet at the head of Grays Harbor, and the harbour, as HistoryLink puts it, "lacked the depth required for large ships" [1]. The explorer who surveyed it in 1841 was unimpressed by its narrow entrance and shallow bottom, suitable only for small vessels [2]. Aberdeen paid for that over decades. A jetty at the entrance, after a failed attempt in 1896, was finished by the Army Corps of Engineers in 1916; a port district was approved in 1911; by the 1920s a channel had been dredged to the town [1].

The pattern recurs across the coast. The Denny party landed at Alki Beach in November 1851 and, finding the anchorage too exposed, moved in April 1852 to the more sheltered eastern shore of Elliott Bay [3]. Port Angeles sits behind Ediz Hook, a long sand spit [4]. At Port Gamble the sand spit at the mouth of the bay "sheltered ships and was close to stands of timber" [5]. Port Townsend stood where every ship from a foreign port had to stop to clear customs, though HistoryLink calls the site the settlers chose "far from ideal": a high bluff separated much of the shore from the uplands [6]. Where deep water was missing, a town paid for it. Olympia, on Budd Inlet, built a wharf 4,798 feet long in 1885 so ships could tie up at any tide; it was needed only until 1895, when the Corps dredged the harbour [7].

On the Willapa River, Raymond "was located at the head of navigable waters, close to the bay and to the forests that fed its mills" [8].

Flat dry ground was the scarce thing, so these towns were built on water. At Port Townsend the planned main street, Water Street, was partly submerged much of the time, and the only buildable lots on the waterfront lay between it and the bluff, about one city block deep [6]. Raymond built 2,900 feet of elevated wooden sidewalks across its water-sodden ground, a visitor described its streets as long bridges on piles driven into the slough, and in 1912 the town began to fill the sloughs so houses would not flood at high tide [8]. Aberdeen's sequence was piles and plank, then sawdust from its mills, then, from 1908, fill taken from a hill that has since been levelled, where the high school stands [1]. Astoria's downtown was built out over the Columbia, first on wooden pilings and later on wooden viaducts that formed its streets [9]. After the 1922 fire, which spread below the paved streets and fed on the creosote on the pilings that held up the pavement [10], the town was rebuilt on dredged sand rather than pilings, with a new layout and wider streets [10]. In all three, pilings came first and fill after, and each changed over on its own date: Aberdeen from 1908, Raymond from 1912 and Astoria after 1922.

Several of these harbours were already Native villages, and the nations are living communities

The settler towns did not go up on empty ground. The research behind this doc began from secondary sources, HistoryLink and the press, and did not consult the nations themselves; for the four named here the claims below were checked against each nation's own published page or against the treaty text, and this doc names no other nation, village or treaty.

Port Angeles is, in HistoryLink's words, "built on the site of two major Klallam villages, I'e'nis and Tse-whit-zen" [4]. The Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe says downtown Port Angeles "was once a thriving Klallam village known as č̕ixʷícən (Tse-whit-zen)", which "flourished for over 2,700 years"; that settlers began arriving in the 1860s and "the homesteaders forced Klallam from their traditional home sites"; and that artifacts and human remains found there in August 2003 made it "the largest pre-European contact village site excavated in Washington State" [11], [12]. As of Nov 22, 2026 the Tribe's page says it owns 1,014 acres and has about 882 enrolled members [12].

At Port Gamble the Port Gamble S'Klallam Tribe records that Captain Josiah Keller of the Puget Mill Company, who arrived in 1853, "saw an excellent location for a sawmill, even though the S'Klallams had long established a village on the site", and that oral history indicates he "incentivized the forced relocation across Port Gamble Bay to Point Julia with offerings of firewood, lumber to build homes, and Christmas 'treats'" [13]. HistoryLink, from the settlers' side, says Keller "induced the natives to move across the bay to Point Julia in exchange for free lumber, firewood, and Christmas gifts" [5].

Treaties decided what stayed in Native hands. The Treaty of Neah Bay, signed on January 31, 1855, has the Makah cede the lands they occupied around Cape Flattery and reserves a tract around Neah Bay and the cape; its Article 4 says "the right of taking fish and of whaling or sealing at usual and accustomed grounds and stations is further secured to said Indians in common with all citizens of the United States" [14]. As of Nov 22, 2026 the Makah Tribe's page calls the tribe "a sovereign nation in its traditional homeland" and says roughly 1,500 live in and around Neah Bay [15]. At La Push, the Quileute Tribe says that after the 1856 Treaty of Olympia and an 1889 executive order it was "confined to the village of La Push, WA, a one-square mile reservation", at the meeting of the Quillayute River and the Pacific. One purpose of Public Law 112-97, enacted February 27, 2012, is to provide the tribe with about 275 acres of land within Olympic National Park and about 510 acres along the Quillayute River; the tribe says it finished the first phase of its move to higher ground, relocating its school, in August 2022 [16], [17].

These are living nations with governments, enrolled members and plans for their own futures, not a layer the settlers' towns replaced. A generated coast that gives each town a "Native village" for atmosphere gets that wrong, and section 7 returns to it.

02

Sawmills founded many of these towns, railroads picked which grew, and the 1889 to 1893 terminus bets left stalled towns

A sawmill came first at Port Gamble, Seattle, Aberdeen and Raymond. Puget Mill Company's partners spotted the sand spit at Port Gamble in the summer of 1853, and by September the mill there was cutting logs [5]. Henry Yesler chose Seattle in the fall of 1852 as the site of Puget Sound's first steam-powered sawmill, in exchange for generous parts of the settlers' claims [3], [18]. Samuel Benn had Aberdeen surveyed and filed a plat in 1884, offering a mill site; A. J. West arrived that June and had his mill running by the next month, and by 1889 Aberdeen had four mills turning out nearly 30 million board feet of lumber [1]. Raymond's land company formed in 1903 and offered free riverfront lots to mills; by 1912 it had 14 mills in operation [8].

Others were founded by an act of government. In 1854 the headquarters of the Puget Sound Customs Collection District moved to Port Townsend [6]. Port Angeles was founded in 1862: Victor Smith won legislation moving the customs port there and got President Lincoln to designate 3,520 acres as a federal reserve for lighthouse, military and naval purposes [4]. A 2011 newspaper account gives 3,640 acres for the reserve [19]; the sources differ, so the range is 3,520 to 3,640 acres. Olympia was a village of about 100 when Isaac Stevens made it the territorial capital in November 1853 [7]. Oysterville's founders arrived in 1854 to file land claims and ship oysters, and vessels from San Francisco soon came for them [20].

Then a railroad's choice could make or starve a port. On July 14, 1873 a crowd at Yesler's mill in Seattle heard a telegram read: "We have located the terminus on Commencement Bay" [21]. Seattle had offered the Northern Pacific 7,500 town lots, 3,000 acres, $50,000 in cash and $200,000 in bonds [21]. Tacoma's population grew from 1,098 in 1880 to 36,006 in 1890; the Northern Pacific built its depot two miles south of the existing town and named it New Tacoma, and the two merged by act of the legislature in January 1884 [22].

The terminus bets of 1889 to 1893 are the other half of the picture. Anacortes had about 40 residents on January 1, 1890, 500 by the end of the month and 2,000 by the end of February, after the Oregon Improvement Company posted $15 million in bonds; the company collapsed in August 1890 and thousands left [23]. South Bend's population rose from about 150 to 3,500 between 1889 and 1894. Its tax valuation went from $2.5 million in 1891 to $414,320 in 1895, and the 400-room Hotel Willapa on the hill above town hosted a single reception, never had an overnight guest because of a loan default, sat empty for nearly three decades and was demolished in 1919 [24]. Port Townsend accepted a Union Pacific offer in 1889 on condition that the city put up $100,000; the groundbreaking was on October 2, 1889, the sponsor went into receivership in November 1890, and the track reached Quilcene and stopped; between 1890 and 1900 nearly one fourth of the people left, leaving fewer than 3,500 [6]. Ocosta, founded in 1890 as the Northern Pacific's intended Grays Harbor terminus, was built mostly over wetlands and tideflats; in 1895 Aberdeen supplanted it as the terminus, after Aberdeen's residents built their own track to the Northern Pacific's line, and Ocosta's businesses all closed [25].

The sources do not count empty platted blocks, and none that this research found does, so this doc does not claim these towns never grew into their plats. What they support is narrower: the line that was promised stopped short or went elsewhere, and the town built for it shrank or stalled.

Two details where sources differ. The county seat of Pacific County is dated differently: HistoryLink has Oysterville holding it from 1855 to 1892, South Bend named the seat in 1892 and South Bend residents forcibly removing the records on February 5, 1893 [20], [24]; the Oregon State Archives lists Chinookville, then Oysterville in 1861, and finally South Bend in 1893 [26]. This doc gives the range, 1892 to 1893, and no single date.

03

Every one was laid out as a grid, and each differs by where its grids collided, what the land broke and what burned

Plats came first and were drawn by whoever owned the ground. In spring 1853 Arthur Denny, Carson Boren and Doc Maynard drew plats for their claims, which abutted Yesler Way. Denny and Boren, HistoryLink says, "believed that streets should generally conform to the topography, aligning their streets with the hills and the waterfront"; Maynard, whose claim was mostly flat, "oriented his grid strictly north-south and east-west" [18]. The plats were filed on May 23, 1853, which "resulted in the tangled intersections still found along Yesler Way" [18]. A local history society adds that all the plats in King County are aligned to true north "except those downtown bits that follow the shoreline" [27]. Seattle's tangle at Yesler Way has a cause a person can name: its claimants could not agree on which way a grid should point [27].

Other plats came from a company laying out a whole town at once. Five hundred men graded, surveyed and platted Everett's townsite by spring 1891, and Hewitt Avenue, a mile and a half long and 100 feet wide, was cut from bayside to riverside [28]. Fairhaven's grid is described by the City of Bellingham as "a conventional compass-oriented grid" with blocks subdivided "without alleys" [29]. At Port Angeles, 600 acres of the federal reserve were cut into lots 50 feet by 140 [19]. In Tacoma, a plan reported in 1873 had three main avenues 100 feet wide parallel to the waterfront and blocks "120 feet deep which ended in 40-foot-wide streets", while a curving plan by Frederick Law Olmsted was dropped 43 days after he presented it; critics likened its blocks to melons and sweet potatoes [30], [31]. Rival plats could also merge: Whatcom, Sehome, Fairhaven and Bellingham were four settlements that became one city in 1904, after the men of Fairhaven and Whatcom voted 2,163 to 596 in 1903 [32].

The land bent the grid. At Port Townsend a high bluff separated much of the shore from the uplands, and the courthouse was built on the bluff [6]. After the fire of 1889 Seattle raised most of its streets by 6.5 to 32 feet, so the ground floors of the first rebuilt buildings became subterranean rooms that were rediscovered in the 1960s [18]. Front and Commercial streets were widened to 84 feet by cutting 9 feet from the property on each side [33].

And fire reset the core. The 1889 fire burned 64 acres according to one HistoryLink essay [3] and 116 acres according to another, which counts 40 blocks plus 18 waterfront blocks [33]; the sources differ, so both are given. After it, "speaker after speaker demanded that only brick and stone be used to rebuild the burned parts of the city" [33]. Aberdeen's downtown burned on October 16, 1903: 140 structures were lost and three people died; HistoryLink puts the burned area at some 200 acres and a Kent Reporter account at seven downtown blocks [1], [34]. It rebuilt in stone and brick [1]. Port Townsend shows the opposite case: after the bust many downtown buildings "sat empty for years, but relatively few were destroyed", and its Historic Landmark District, established in 1977, is one of three surviving Victorian-era seaports in the nation [6].

That is the history. The next question is what the streets themselves say, which is the part of the research that was measured.

04

Of 21 towns measured, 9 have two or three street grids, and median blocks run from 249 by 259 to 372 by 560 feet

This section is the doc's own data: 20 Washington towns and Astoria, Oregon, measured from OpenStreetMap, which is © OpenStreetMap contributors, available under the Open Database Licence (ODbL) [35]. The data was downloaded on Nov 22, 2026; OpenStreetMap is edited live, so a later download will differ.

Method. For each town a bounding box was drawn by hand around the built-up area, and the script kept the streets a car can drive on (residential, unclassified, tertiary, secondary, primary, trunk, their link types and living streets, not service roads, tracks or paths), projected them to UTM zone 10N and clipped them to the box. Bearings are weighted by street length, folded into 0 to 90 degrees in one-degree bins and smoothed. A "grid" is a peak whose five-degree window holds at least 8% of all street length and at least twice the average density of the bearings left unassigned; each grid is credited with all street within eight degrees of its peak. Blocks are the faces made by noding the street centre lines and polygonising; "near-rectangular" means a face area at least 85% of its smallest enclosing rectangle, with both sides 30 to 400 metres. A script did the measuring and re-running it from its cached downloads gave byte-identical output.

Limits. These carry into every number below. Block sizes are centre line to centre line, so each includes one street width, half on each side; they are not platted block or lot sizes. Dead-end streets bound no block. Tagging is uneven: alleys are often not mapped, and a missing alley tag does not mean there is no alley. The boxes were chosen by hand, so per-area and size figures depend on them. Blocks at the box edge are open and not counted. Rivers are compared by the bank nearest the town's place point and meander, so those angles are local. Building counts were also made, but mapping is uneven and some towns' counts look incomplete (Astoria's does), so they are not used here. Where fewer than 10 near-rectangular blocks were found, block size is not given.

What the table shows:

  • Grids. Twelve towns show one grid (Astoria, Raymond, Montesano, Ilwaco, Long Beach, Ocean Shores, Oysterville, Tokeland, McCleary, Pe Ell, Forks and Naselle), seven show two and two show three (Port Townsend and Port Gamble). Because a grid's streets run at right angles, a second grid 54 degrees from the first is the same as one 36 degrees the other way, and the figure shows the smaller angle. In five of the seven two-grid towns (Aberdeen, Hoquiam, South Bend, Cosmopolis and Westport) it is 37 to 44 degrees; in Port Angeles it is 30 and in Elma 23.
  • Which grids follow the shore. Port Angeles's dominant grid (60% of its streets, at 32 degrees) runs within 3 degrees of the local shoreline, and Tokeland's at 30 degrees does the same.   • Blocks. Medians run from 249 by 259 feet in Long Beach and 253 by 361 in Astoria to 372 by 560 in Port Angeles, whose most common block is 370 by 570 feet (57 blocks); Aberdeen's most common are about 330 to 370 feet square.
  • Alleys are tagged in 90% of Port Angeles's blocks and 75% of Aberdeen's, but in 1% of Port Townsend's and none of Long Beach's. The data does not say whether the low figures mean no alleys or unmapped ones.
  • The highway. In 16 of the 21 towns a trunk, primary or secondary road runs through the town, within about 220 metres of the place point; in Montesano, Elma and McCleary it runs at the edge, 370 to 520 metres off; at Tokeland the road tagged is a bypass 2,396 metres away, and Astoria and Elma also have a bypass. No such road is tagged inside the Ocean Shores box.
  • Rail. In Aberdeen, Hoquiam, Port Angeles and Raymond a railway line, tagged abandoned, lies between the centre point and the water, 16 to 165 metres from the shore; in Montesano a line lies 564 metres from the water.

The measurements say where a grid points and how large a block is. They say nothing about why, which is what the history was for.

05

Small towns come in kinds, each with one reason to exist

Oyster towns. Oysterville, on the bay side of the Long Beach Peninsula, was founded in 1854 for oysters; HistoryLink says it was the county seat from 1855 to 1892, and pictures its 1892 church, its 1907 schoolhouse and a post office it calls the oldest in Washington run continuously under the same name (1858) [20]. Willapa Bay is called the "Oyster Capital of the World" and is thought to be the largest farmed shellfish producer in the United States, with an estimated 350 independent growers [20]. Lewis Loomis built a narrow-gauge railroad from Ilwaco to Nahcotta in 1888, and it took its final run on September 10, 1930 [20]. My reading, not a sourced claim: Oysterville kept its old buildings and lost its trade when the railroad and the county seat went elsewhere.

Cannery and fishing harbours. Ilwaco's boom came after 1882 with salmon traps of tarred rope web on permanent pilings, which took its population to 300 and started a conflict with gillnet fishers [20]. In Wahkiakum County on the lower Columbia, canneries had built their reputation on a prime product that had run out, and the last closed in 1947 [36]. At Westport, a basin was dredged along the shore soon after World War II; by the end of the 1940s the marina's business kept two stores, two fish-packing plants, a tavern and a restaurant going, and Army Corps floats took the docking space to 646 berths in the 1950s and early 1960s [37].

River landings. Wahkiakum County's towns lived by steamboat and schooner: no road joined Cathlamet and Skamokawa until 1917, and when the river trade ended, places off the highway such as Brookfield and Altoona became more isolated, not less [36].

Beach resorts. Tourists began arriving at Long Beach in the late nineteenth century; HistoryLink says it triples in population each July and August [20]. The research expected railways to have made the resorts, but the pages that could be opened do not say so for Long Beach, and the sources' dates for Moclips conflict, so this doc names no resort as rail-made.

Company mill towns. Port Gamble's founders planned it "to reflect the character of their hometown, East Machias, Maine"; the mill closed in 1995 after 142 years, and the townsite is preserved as a National Historic Site [5]. Its single workers lived in a bunkhouse and its families in small cabins [5]. McCleary's founder sold "the mill and the town" to Simpson Logging on December 31, 1941, and Simpson sold the light and water plants to the new city for $6,000 the following year [38].

County seats. Charles Byles platted Montesano in 1870; in 1886 it won the county seat from a bid by Cosmopolis, and Byles donated the land for the courthouse [2]. South Bend, as above, took Pacific County's seat in 1892 to 1893 and built a courthouse in 1910 to 1911 [24].

Towns that failed. Ocosta is the clean case. It was a boomtown built over tideflats for a terminus it lost; its businesses all closed and the downtown "disappeared from the marsh and tideflats", and the State of Washington opened Bottle Beach State Park on the site of downtown Ocosta in 2009 [25].

06

Public ports still ship while the mills have closed, and the decline left some of the old town standing

This section describes the places as of Nov 22, 2026, when it was written. Grays Harbor's public port followed the Port District Act, signed on March 14, 1911: voters of what was then Chehalis County, renamed Grays Harbor in 1915, voted to form a countywide port district on December 12, 1911 [39]. As of Nov 22, 2026 the port's page says its largest marine terminal customer, AGP, runs the largest soymeal exporter on the West Coast at Terminal 2 and will invest in a second export ship-loading facility at Terminal 4 [40].

Mills have closed in several of these towns, and some of their sites are still being cleaned up. At Port Angeles, the Rayonier mill property covers about 70 acres on the Strait of Juan de Fuca; Rayonier operated the pulp mill until February 1997, and Ecology is supervising its cleanup [41]. The same page says Ennis Creek runs through the site and that the area is important to the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe, whose village I'e'nis stood on the creek's eastern bank [41]. In 2025 Ecology asked for public comment on a consent decree and an interim action plan for the site [42]. In Bellingham, Georgia-Pacific shut its pulp mill in 2001, and in 2005 the Port of Bellingham acquired the 137-acre property to rebuild the waterfront economy [43].

The measured data shows another layer. In Aberdeen, Hoquiam, Port Angeles and Raymond a railway tagged abandoned runs between the centre point and the water (section 4). That is only what OpenStreetMap's editors recorded; it does not say what the line carried.

Some of what the decline left was kept. Port Townsend's brick downtown, left empty by the bust, is in a Historic Landmark District established in 1977 [6]. Port Gamble's mill closed in 1995 and the town is preserved as a company town [5]. In the other direction, as of November 2021 the state counted two tsunami vertical evacuation structures in Washington, one at Ocosta Elementary School near Westport and one then under construction at Shoalwater Bay near Tokeland, and its assessment said Pacific County needs between 25 and 40 more and Grays Harbor between 30 and 42 [44]. The Quileute Tribe, for its part, finished moving its school out of the tsunami zone in August 2022 [16].

07

For a generator: decide the town's history first, let the land set the grids, and vary what a person can name

This section is the application, not the subject, and it is short. This research was done for Cape Sorrow, a game in development at games.csarko.sh/capesorrow in which a detective investigates reports across a generated coastal Washington. As of Nov 22, 2026 its generator for the large port draws a history first, the founding purpose (sawmill, cannery, customs or railhead) and the plats, and derives the streets and blocks from it; only the port kind exists [45].

A history first. The published methods generate geometry. Parish and Müller's 2001 system takes image maps of land and water boundaries and population density and generates streets, lots and buildings from them [46]; Chen and colleagues' 2008 system has a designer edit a tensor field that steers the street network [47]; Galin and colleagues' 2010 roads follow a least-cost path that weighs terrain slope and obstacles such as rivers and lakes [48]; Weber and colleagues' 2009 system simulates an urban model over time at about one second a step [49]. None of them produces a cause: a founding purpose, a plat that was drawn to one bearing, a fire. A real town's irregularity has one, as sections 2 and 3 show, and a generator that draws the cause first can show a player where it came from.

The land sets the grids. The coast's towns were grids, not organic growth. Their irregularity came from plats that collided, bluffs and tideflats. Section 4's data points the same way: 9 of 21 towns have two or three grids, and a dominant grid follows the shore in only two (Port Angeles and Tokeland), the compass in eight and cannot be told from either in eight more. A generator can give each plat its own bearing and let the shore and the bluff cut the blocks.

Variation a person cannot name is the failure. Kate Compton calls it the 10,000 bowls of oatmeal problem: a generator can make 10,000 bowls of oatmeal in which each oat sits in a different place, "mathematically speaking they will all be completely unique", but "the user will likely just see a lot of oatmeal"; perceptual uniqueness is "the real metric" [50]. Rabii and Cook's 2023 paper proves a relationship between the Kolmogorov complexity of the most complex artifact a generator can produce and the size of its possibility space, and so links the intricacy of what it makes to the knowledge encoded in it [51]. The variation worth paying for is the kind a player can say in a sentence: the town that never recovered when the rail line went to the next harbour. Jitter on block size over one rule set is the oatmeal.

The nations. A generated town should not carry a Native village as scenery. A nation's community has its own place, plan and enterprises, and, as section 1 shows, may be moving uphill for the same tsunami reasons as its neighbours. The nations' own sources should be read before a game names or depicts one.
